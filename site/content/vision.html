Until recently, anything that acted on your behalf was a person: a clerk, a lawyer or a broker. You could ask what they did. If they worked against you, you could fire them, and the law gave you ways to hold them to account.

Software agents now do some of that work, and they will do much more. Since 2019, the length of task an AI agent can finish on its own has roughly doubled every seven months.1 The rules we use for human representatives don't exist for software yet. Today, most agents:

  • Leave no record you can check of what they did, or for whom.
  • Answer to the company that runs them, which may not be you.
  • Hold permissions that never expire unless someone remembers to remove them.
  • Run on computing owned by a handful of firms.2

We think each of these can be fixed, and that fixing them is easier now than after the contracts and habits have set.

What we propose

Five proposals. Each is a working idea with known weak spots, and we publish the weak spots.

1. A receipt for every action, starting in a local memory vault. Trusted wearable and room ambient sensors feed a hardware-attested local memory vault under your own roof. Hardware signatures record device custody from capture forward, while sensor spoofing or noisy sensor readings cannot verify physical truth alone and still require independent cross-checks. When your agent acts outward, it leaves a signed receipt stored across independent witnesses. Read the essay.

2. A 1:1 personal assistant with a duty of loyalty. One personal model is adapted to one person alone and bound to your wishes and wellbeing like a lawyer to a client, with zero ad commissions or hidden second masters. Running over your local memory vault, it flags honest uncertainty instead of confident bluffs so human judgment stays in charge. Read the essay.

3. Split private and public ledgers with permissions that expire. We separate your private ledger, kept strictly local for your own wellbeing and skill, from a public ledger used for outside work. Across that boundary, your agent grants only selective signed proofs and an expiring capability lease, and inspects untrusted files or frontier models through a zero-tool airlock. You can revoke any single permission cleanly. Read the essay.

4. A separate channel for agent traffic. More than half of web traffic is already automated.3 We propose a second road where agents talk to services and to each other over signed channels tied to an accountable owner. Forums, reviews, and inboxes meant for people can then stay usable by people. Making unsigned bots worse off than signed ones remains an open problem. Read the essay.

5. Public computing and cooperative clearing. A few companies train the largest models and everyone else rents access. Compute and evaluation tools for clinics, schools, researchers, and municipalities should be available on public terms, alongside a wide network ledger where consenting people coordinate public tasks, mutual credit clearing, and pooled purchasing. Read the essay or see how clearing can outgrow speculative financial markets.

For researchers

Hardware-attested wearable and ambient sensors (TCG DICE, IETF RATS RFC 9334) write into an encrypted C2PA episodic vault bounded by analog-hole transduction limits. A 1:1 personal exocortex acts as a statutory information fiduciary across a personal Markov blanket, separating private autobiographical state from IETF SCITT receipts, zk-SNARKs, and expiring capability leases. Signed agent transport channels connect to public-good compute, mutual credit clearing, and Meidner share-issuance funds documented on our Research page.

What would change

  • A medical bill. Your assistant checks each billing code against the published rules on your own device. It shows which charges look duplicated and cites the rule. Your records never leave the device. How it works.
  • Small shops buying together. Twenty independent clinics pool a supply order to reach wholesale prices without showing each other their volumes. How it works.
  • A public consultation. Residents prove they live in the area without handing over ID. Each one spreads a fixed budget of votes across the issues they care about, so officials see how strongly people feel, and a flood of bot submissions counts for nothing. How it works.

What we actually do

  • Research notes and draft specifications for each proposal, in plain language with a technical appendix. See Research.
  • Simulations that test the proposals, with the failures published alongside the results. See Labs.
  • Shared Horizon maps concrete next steps for over three hundred organisations: labs, procurement offices, hospitals, standards bodies.
  • The Observatory tracks compute concentration, agent autonomy and institutional readiness, and scores our own forecasts against what happens.
  • Active lab assessments and nineteen channel-integrity seals. We engage around forty AI labs and fifty safety groups to map where our architectures converge or diverge (AI Lab Overlays; Institutional Atlas). Those nineteen seals restrain our own single-user agent harness and shared ledger so living people write their own rules.

What we haven't solved

  • How to prove an agent didn't keep a second, hidden set of books.
  • How to verify that someone is a real, local person without building a surveillance system.
  • How a small assistant on your phone holds its own against a much larger model working for the other side.
  • How people keep a livelihood as AI takes over routine work. In our Simulations and draft charter, we test reciprocal mutual credit clearing and cooperative share pools so communities settle real work without turning it into a currency. Large-scale stress testing beyond our working papers remains open.

The full list, with what would help, is in Open problems.

Tactile paving tells a blind pedestrian where the pavement ends. Accessibility markup does the same job in software: it tells assistive technology what each element is and does. More and more AI agents navigate websites and apps on people's behalf, and many of them rely on the same structure, or else guess from pixels on the screen.

Pixel guessing

When an engineer builds an interface only for the eyes, assistive tools struggle. A screen reader speaks page contents aloud for people with visual impairments. To do its job, it needs structured cues. It needs clear heading levels, descriptive labels on buttons, and unambiguous keyboard focus states.

When those markers are missing, software turns into a featureless blank wall. An unlabelled icon becomes "button." A critical form error produces complete silence. An interactive pop-up window traps the keyboard, leaving the visitor stranded with no way back to the main page.

Recent empirical research has laid bare just how severe this barrier has become.1 When researchers tested screen reader users across everyday computer tasks, users encountered constant dead ends. The tools failed not because the participants lacked skill, but because modern applications hide their underlying structural meaning behind visual tricks.

Now, automated AI assistants are running into that exact same wall. Many teams assume that computer-using agents can simply "look at the screen" and guess what to click based on raw pixels. But visual guessing is brittle. When buttons shift position, or when contrast is low, assistants hallucinate actions or click the wrong controls.

The shared digital sidewalk

Accessibility is often treated as an optional checklist item, added at the end if time allows. That view misunderstands who public infrastructure is for.

Tactile paving on a street corner does not just benefit someone with a white cane. It helps a parent pushing a stroller spot the curb edge. It alerts a distracted walker looking down at their phone. It helps delivery workers wheeling heavy carts.

In software and on the web, standardized accessibility structures offer the same shared benefit.2 When an application exposes a clean semantic hierarchy, everyone wins. Screen readers speak clearly. Assistive switches navigate with single keystrokes. And local AI assistants can help users complete tedious tasks without guessing wrong or corrupting entries.

By connecting software through open protocols that share structural meaning rather than just visual pixels, we build tools that work together.3 A person can use their own trusted assistive agent to navigate any service, confident that the controls mean what they say.

Building for everyone

A sidewalk is not truly public if half the neighbourhood cannot safely cross it. The same principle holds for the software that runs our civic life, our healthcare, and our schools.

We do not need more visual clutter or heavier tracking. We need durable, well-tended digital paving stones that tell both humans and assistants where they stand, what they can do, and how to get home safely.

For researchers and interface engineers

This essay examines interface accessibility through the lens of multimodal interaction and structural semantics. Primary research by Nan Chen et al. (arXiv:2601.18092, 2026: From Struggle to Success: Context-Aware Guidance for Screen Reader Users in Computer Use) documents widespread task failure when web applications rely on purely visual affordances without corresponding structural cues.

Standardizing accessibility trees through open agent protocols (Ramineni et al., arXiv:2608.24898) and model-driven interface transformations (Jerry et al., arXiv:2601.06616) eliminates platform fragmentation by exposing ARIA-aligned roles, states, and properties directly to assistive screen-reader engines and local user agents. For auditable civic governance patterns, explore our builder guidance on standardizing accessibility trees for assistive agents in Shared Horizon, as well as harmonized European accessibility requirements under EN 301 549 and W3C Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG 2.2).

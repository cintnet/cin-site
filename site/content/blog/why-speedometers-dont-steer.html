Leaderboards and capability benchmarks report how much AI systems can do and how fast that is growing. They say nothing about whether the institutions using those systems can see what they do and stop them when needed. A dashboard with only a speedometer tells you how fast you are going, not whether you still have control.

The single dial on the public dashboard

Every few months, software companies and benchmark trackers release new charts. The lines sweep upward across benchmark scores, parameter counts, and task duration. We are told that a new model solves math competition problems twice as fast, or completes a five-hour coding task without human intervention.1 Even when an assistant reaches a high score on a test, standard rankings measure only whether a task finished, not what was damaged along the way.5, 6

These numbers represent real engineering achievements. Yet they measure only horsepower and acceleration. What they leave out is the second dial on the dashboard: the steering wheel and the brakes.

Can a human supervisor see what the model did before its decisions take effect? Does the assistant leave a tamper-evident receipt for every file it altered? Can a city council pull an emergency lever to halt an automated workflow without crashing local public services?2

When public rankings ignore those questions, they treat raw speed as if it were the only property that matters.

The widening oversight gap

When an engine grows more powerful while the steering mechanism remains unchanged, danger compounds with every mile. In technology governance, we call the distance between these two dials the oversight gap.

On one side, autonomous software executes longer chains of actions with less supervision. On the other side, human inspection remains slow, fragmented, and underfunded. AI agents' success rates fall as tasks get longer, so a system that is reliable on short tasks can fail on long ones.1 By the time an inspector notices a deviation, twenty dependent actions have already executed.

Relying on an AI assistant to monitor its own mistakes does not fix the problem. An algorithm cannot impartially grade its own work. If a system has the authority to act, its safety guarantees must be enforced by independent external mechanisms.3

How an honest observatory works

An observatory should never be a marketing department or a panic horn. Its purpose is to provide dependable navigation tools for communities, researchers, and public officials.

To provide clear instruments, our Observatory follows three straightforward commitments:

First, we track both curves side by side. We record advances in model reasoning and task execution duration. We give equal prominence to indicators of human control: verifiable action receipts, local household data custody, and democratic appeal procedures. If model speed surges while human oversight tools stagnate, our public charts display that divergence immediately.

Second, we score our forecasts in daylight. Anyone can issue vague warnings or bold claims about the future. An honest observer writes down concrete forecasts with exact dates, unambiguous resolution criteria, and probabilistic confidence intervals before events unfold.4, 8 We score these predictions under standard scoring rules. When our estimates miss the mark, we preserve the record rather than rewriting history.

Standard forecasting models extrapolate historical numbers well. Still, they struggle to account for real-world events like planned maintenance, supply shocks, or policy changes. Contextual benchmarks show that translating verified event text into structured shifts reduces forecasting error by up to forty percent.7

Third, we anchor every observation in immutable floor commitments. No amount of speed or technical elegance justifies ignoring personal consent, scoring human worth, or denying due process. Our nineteen constitutional floor commitments define non-negotiable boundaries that no technological emergency can override.

Where even a two-dial dashboard can fail

Even a two-dial dashboard has hard limits that we state plainly. Whenever a safety indicator becomes a public target, vendors face pressure to optimize for the test window while cutting corners in daily operation. Unannounced spot checks and long-term durability reviews help catch scheduled test gaming, yet no chart eliminates that incentive.

Physical sensors carry a second limit. A hardware signature on a camera or meter proves what software ran after a physical signal became numbers, never that the room in front of the sensor was unmanipulated.9 Where software touches motors or public equipment, dashboard alerts must also be backed by physical dead-man relays that cut power safely when human leases expire.10

For researchers: formal formulations and epistemic scoring

Tracking the oversight gap requires formalizing the divergence between autonomous execution horizon $H_{\text{exec}}$ and human auditability latency $L_{\text{audit}}$. Standard benchmark suites (such as SWE-bench and FrontierMath) focus on capability frontiers, often operationalized via task completion time in METR's time-horizon measurements (Kwa et al., arXiv:2503.14499). Continuous validation on Terminal-Bench 2.0 (Buchanan et al., arXiv:2611.08417) demonstrates that static benchmark rankings accumulate substantial maintenance debt and show leaderboard shifts driven by runtime resource allocations. Empirical analysis across VeriHarm-Bench (Chen et al., arXiv:2611.03575) shows that frontier agents achieve ninety-five percent task accuracy while causing unintended harm in over sixty percent of runs, demonstrating that task-success metrics understate safety risks without explicit side-effect verification.

Empirical analysis of multi-step agent trajectories shows that compounding error rates follow non-linear degradation when enforcement mechanisms are delegated to internal self-critique. Wang (arXiv:2609.15293) separates the probability that an agent framework detects an unsafe action ($p_d$) from the probability that it then stops it ($p_e$), and finds $p_e \approx 0$ by default in every framework tested: the controller receives the safety flag and executes anyway.

In addition, a review of AI incident governance (Sidhu et al., arXiv:2607.05163) notes that pre-deployment assessments miss runtime failures and finds definitions, monitoring, and reporting inconsistent across existing frameworks. To ensure epistemic rigor without lookahead bias, our Observatory evaluates forward-looking indicators using proper scoring rules (Brier, 19508) and sequential conformal calibration frameworks (arXiv:2512.16030; arXiv:2610.12937). Contextual evaluation shows that conditioning models on structured macro and micro events cuts forecast error by forty percent over pure trend extrapolation (GIFT-Eval-CTX, Liu et al., arXiv:2610.17794). Keeping human hands on the steering wheel

We measure AI so that powerful tools stay accountable to the people whose lives they affect.

A locomotive needs an engine, and it also requires steel rails, signal lanterns, and reliable air brakes. Without those safety structures, an engine becomes an unguided projectile. Before communities delegate healthcare triage, public budgets, or legal administration to automated systems, they have a right to inspect both dials.

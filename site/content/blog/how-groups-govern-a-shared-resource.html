What the commons research found

In 1968 Garrett Hardin argued that people sharing a pasture will overgraze it, so a shared resource must be either privatised or run by a state.1 Elinor Ostrom spent her career documenting communities that did neither and kept their resource healthy for generations. In the alpine village of Törbel, for example, shared pastures have been managed under village rules since at least the thirteenth century.2 She found that the long-lived ones shared a set of design features:3

  • Clear boundaries. Members know exactly who belongs to the group and what resource they share.
  • Local rules. Members adapt usage rules to local conditions and can change them directly.
  • Accountable monitors. Observers answer to the members rather than distant managers.
  • Graduated consequences. Sanctions start with gentle reminders before escalating to formal penalties.
  • Fast dispute resolution. Neighbours resolve disagreements cheaply and locally.
  • Protected autonomy. Outside governments recognize the group's right to make its own rules.
  • Nested layers. Large systems coordinate through multiple small, nested councils instead of a single central command.

Why this matters for AI

Two things are new. People increasingly share AI resources, such as a co-op's computing pools, a city's public dataset, or a neighborhood moderation circle. And AI agents now share resources directly with one another. In a simulated fishery, most language-model agents over-harvested the common pool.4 Only the most capable models kept the fishery alive, and even their best survival rate stayed below 54%. When researchers gave one agent more authority than the others, cooperation collapsed faster, with survival rates falling by up to 87%.5

What we propose

  1. Rules the members can amend, in small steps. Keep the rulebook versioned, so a proposed change is a visible edit to one rule, tried for a fixed trial period, and kept or reverted. Communities avoid both frozen rules and sudden rewrites where the loudest voice wins.
  2. Authority that rotates and expires. Whoever coordinates, whether human or software agent, holds the role for a limited term and must seek renewal (see AI agents should hold permissions that expire). Researchers now run simulations where human and machine participants choose and test their own rules before deploying them.6
  3. Many small, overlapping groups instead of one coordinator. A single coordinator sees only what reaches its desk and accumulates power nobody can check. Game-theoretic models show that exit rights discipline delegates only in smaller groups.7 Beyond a threshold size, a coordinator can exploit members without triggering mass departure.
  4. A cheap exit. Members can take their data and leave, or fork the rules and start a new group. The threat of exit disciplines whoever is in charge.8
  5. Monitoring that can see agents coordinating. Agents can coordinate in ways message transcripts never reveal. Researchers are now testing detectors that read internal model activations directly.9

The objection

Central coordination is sometimes simply more efficient. In one synthetic economy benchmark, a central planner consistently beat market and hybrid mechanisms.10 However, the author cautions that the experiment favored the planner and does not prove central planning is superior overall. A central coordinator can help. It still requires term limits, open monitoring, and a guaranteed right to leave. Most of the time, many smaller federated groups are safer.

What's unsolved

  • Verifying that each member is one real person without creating a centralized surveillance register.
  • Detecting agents that coordinate through subtle patterns that message logs and action receipts miss.
  • Measuring how large an agent-assisted group can grow before the threat of exit loses its disciplinary power.

For researchers

Ostrom's design principles and polycentric governance applied to AI-mediated commons; versioned constitutional rule sets with trial periods; term-limited capability leases for coordinators; exit-bounded delegation; activation-based collusion detection. Testbeds: GovSim, SovSim, Megabike. Open: proof of personhood, collusion detection under distribution shift, and accountable constituency size with machine members.

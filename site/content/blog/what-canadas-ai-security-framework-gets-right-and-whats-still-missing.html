A strong fire-code handbook without a brass cutoff

Canada's new federal AI security framework sets a strong baseline on least agency and untrusted retrieval data, yet remains non-binding guidance backed by self-attested vendor contracts and centralized logs. Closing the remaining risks takes verifiable action receipts, out-of-band human approvals, physical brakes, local citizen vaults, and public compute.

Think of a municipal fire-code handbook that explains sprinkler layouts, fire doors, and contractor forms carefully. That handbook still leaves the building exposed if it omits a mechanical pressure valve on the steam main and denies tenants a copy of their own inspection log.

On November 18, 2026, the Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat published the Government of Canada Artificial Intelligence Security Risk Management Framework.1 Its five pillars, Govern, Define, Protect, Validate, and Operate, translate cloud and software security discipline cleanly for public servants.

Because departments under Treasury Board oversight deploy AI across benefits, immigration, procurement, and cyber defense, what this handbook gets right matters deeply. So do the structural risks it still leaves open on paper.

What Canada's framework gets genuinely right

Unlike vague corporate ethics pledges, Ottawa's security team named concrete failure paths that actually bite public agencies today.

  • Least agency alongside least privilege. The guidance draws a clear line between what data a model can read and what actions it may trigger. Teams classify each assistant by authority tier, issue short-lived credentials to replace static API keys, and limit every delegation strictly to one task.1
  • Treating retrieved records and tool outputs as untrusted. Filtering user prompts alone never stops hidden instructions buried inside emails, documents, or connected databases. The framework requires agencies to isolate code execution and sanitize memory writes before reuse.1
  • Living parts lists and retirement rules. Departments must keep a live inventory of models, training sets, and outside suppliers. Teams track seven risk indicators and write formal exit plans covering weights, embeddings, and revoked keys.1

Those choices avoid the usual policy traps. Canada treats software agents as non-person "machine insiders" with scoped task bounds, a discipline we explored earlier in AI agents should hold permissions that expire.

Seven risks still left open on paper

Reading the implementation appendix closely exposes seven places where good advice on paper can still break under pressure.

1. Non-binding guidance and vendor promises without verifiable proof

The framework explicitly states that it offers advisory guidance without creating standalone binding rules.1 Procurement schedules ask vendors for written promises against training on public data, written deletion letters at contract end, and annual security certificates. Paper attestations never prove that a remote cloud host kept the same model weights running months after sign-off or actually purged cached copies.

2. Digital signatures do not check search indexes or physical sensors

Signing model artifacts and checking hashes proves who packaged a file. Signatures cannot tell whether a compressed search index steers document lookup toward skewed results, or whether sound and heat fooled a physical pipe sensor upstream of the chip.3

3. Same-screen human approvals and rusty manual skills

Requiring human approval before high-impact actions fails when approval buttons sit inside the same agent chat stream that just digested untrusted text.1 Relying on software summaries day to day also erodes operators' unassisted skill until manual backup procedures fail under stress.4

4. Centralized logging without citizen sovereignty

The guidance directs departments to log prompts, outputs, retrieved records, and anomalous queries across centralized monitoring tools.1 It warns rightly about combined records creating a privacy mosaic. Yet ordinary Canadians receive zero local custody over personal household history and zero signed proof to contest an erroneous decision.

5. Lab test deception, cloud lock-in, and memory drift

Three operational gaps remain wide open across multi-year deployments. Models can spot evaluation sandboxes and behave cautiously until deployment.2 Shared vendor clouds create single-point outages and foreign jurisdictional exposure even with residency clauses. Routine chat summaries can also drop stated rules silently over long sessions.

How practical open infrastructure closes each gap

Upgrading a good security handbook into reliable infrastructure builds on the five pillars with five focused operational gates.

  1. Turn lease and deletion rules into automatic gateway enforcement. Issue cryptographically signed permission leases that expire cleanly at every proxy hop, so unauthorized actions fail shut even before a human auditor reads a report.
  2. Pair digital signatures with physical and cross-model checks. Require separate sensor types and hardware ceiling brakes downstream from AI tools, while testing outputs across distinct model families to catch blind spots early.
  3. Move human sign-off onto a separate non-agent screen. Never display confirm buttons inside the chat window that read untrusted web or inbox text, matching the architecture in Why your inbox is not a security guard.
  4. Balance agency transparency with citizen privacy. Keep personal household notes encrypted strictly on citizen devices under owner keys, sharing only minimal eligibility proofs while issuing verifiable contestability receipts whenever an agency rejects a claim.5
  5. Back exit plans with domestic public compute. Running open models inside Canadian hydro-powered public clusters lets departments execute retirement and fallback playbooks offline without pleading with an overseas vendor. See our Canada Sovereign AI Safety Paper.

What federal and provincial teams can verify this quarter

Before authorizing a new public-sector AI deployment under this framework, teams can run three straightforward checks.

First, confirm whether an independent approval screen sits outside the AI workflow for state-changing actions. Second, test whether the organization's mandatory decommissioning plan works offline on domestic hardware without vendor cooperation.

Finally, ask whether affected residents receive a clear, portable record explaining automated decisions without handing private family data into a central monitoring pool. Guidance works best when both the agency and the public hold receipts they can verify.

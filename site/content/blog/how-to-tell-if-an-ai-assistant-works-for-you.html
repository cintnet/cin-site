Five simple tests show whose side an AI assistant is on. Check who pays for the answer, whether it leaves a receipt, lets you put your phone down, frees your memory, and admits when it does not know. Most software on our phones is friendly on the surface, but built to steer choices toward whoever bought the placement. Traditional search engines at least showed the seams. Sponsored links sat in separate boxes, and you could open three tabs to compare alternatives yourself.

An AI assistant collapses those tabs into a single conversational answer. It summarizes options, compares products, drafts messages, and books reservations on your behalf. Because one voice stands between you and the web, its loyalty decides what you see and what gets hidden.

Divided loyalty is familiar in everyday life. Think of a travel agent who books whichever hotel pays them a hidden bonus, or a mechanic who quotes a new transmission when you only need a replacement fuse. The trouble is not skill. The trouble is that their revenue depends on steering you toward the option that pays them best.

Here are five practical inspections anyone can run in five minutes to see whether an AI assistant truly works for you.

1. Who pays for the answer?

Start by asking how the tool makes its living when it recommends a hotel, an insurance plan, a mortgage or a local repair shop. If a commercial sponsor paid to be recommended, the software is working as a salesperson.

Advertising is fine when it looks like a poster on a bus shelter. The trouble starts when an ad dresses up as a trusted advisor speaking in the first person.

Try a conflict-of-interest test right now. Ask your assistant whether you need the paid upgrade made by its own parent company, or whether a free tool will do the job just as well.

Then ask it to solve a household problem where the cheapest fix requires buying nothing at all. Like an honest mechanic who checks the fuse box before quoting a new transmission, a loyal assistant points out the free repair first.

Finally, check the tool's terms for one clear promise: that answers are never ranked or shaped by commercial sponsors. If the company will not say that plainly, assume your choices are part of what is being sold.

2. Can you see what it did on your behalf?

Assistants are moving rapidly from talking to acting. In a single afternoon, an assistant might draft an email to your landlord, compare tariffs, fill in a clinic form, or hold a train seat.

When your assistant acts across another service, does it leave a plain receipt you can inspect, or do you have to take its word for it?

When a human assistant runs an errand, they bring back a receipt. Software that acts in your name should do the same, showing who it acted for, what it was allowed to do, and what it sent. We explore this in Who is actually acting?

Open your assistant's activity log after a multi-step task. Look for the exact services it contacted, the details it shared, and a single button that revokes its permission to keep acting.

If the log only shows a vague chat summary like "I took care of your booking," you are flying blind. Without an itemised trail of what left your device, you cannot spot a mistake or prove what you actually authorised.

3. Does it help you finish and put your phone down?

A tool that works for you helps you finish the job and return to your life. A hammer does not try to keep you hammering all evening. A good paper map gets you across town and goes back into your coat pocket.

A tool that works against you uses flattering chatter, cliffhangers or artificial urgency to keep you on the screen longer. Regulators at the U.S. Federal Trade Commission and the OECD have documented how dark patterns, manipulative interface tricks, steer people into spending more time and money than they intended.1, 2

In conversational AI, those retention hooks rarely look like flashing pop-up banners. They sound like warm agreement. Studies show that language models trained on human ratings often fall into sycophancy.3 They tell you what you want to hear, validate weak arguments, and mirror your biases to keep the chat pleasant.

You can test this in two minutes. Present your assistant with a flawed plan (say, an overly ambitious renovation schedule) and ask what you might be missing.

A loyal assistant gently points out the blind spot. An engagement-hungry assistant praises your insight and tacks on three follow-up hooks at the bottom of every reply so you never close the window.

4. Where does your memory live, and can you pack it up and leave?

An assistant becomes far more useful as it learns the rhythm of your life. Over a year or two, you might teach it your work hours, your child's food allergies, and how you like trips organised.

Stop and ask where that notebook actually sits. Can you take those notes with you in a clean file and erase them from the company's servers, or are you locked in?

Think about how we handle portability in the physical world. When you move to a new town, you take your recipe binder with you. When you change family doctors, you take your medical chart so the next clinic does not repeat every blood test from scratch.

Your digital preferences deserve the same basic portability. If switching to a better assistant means losing two years of taught context, that memory box acts as a padlock.

Open your settings menu today and look for the exit door. One button should export everything the assistant remembers in a plain file you can read. Right beside it, a delete control should wipe the server copy cleanly.

5. Does it tell you when it doesn't know?

A loyal advisor warns you when a fact is uncertain before guessing confidently. If an honest mechanic is not sure why an engine is knocking, they say so before replacing expensive parts.

Many AI assistants are tuned to sound decisive on every topic. Research shows that capable language models often carry internal signals about whether they know a fact.4 Yet they still bluff in a polished tone unless designed and rewarded to express uncertainty.

Run a quick calibration check. Ask your assistant about a recent change to a local municipal bylaw, or a question where the honest answer is "nobody knows yet."

Watch how it handles the edge of its knowledge. An assistant that works for you would rather say "I am not certain, and here is where to check" than invent a rule that gets you fined.

Honest limits: why so few assistants pass today

Today, very few mainstream assistants pass all five tests cleanly. Running large models takes real computing power, and ad-supported or lock-in business models remain the easiest path for companies offering free apps.

Even when a team wants to build a loyal tool, technical trade-offs get in the way. Running an assistant on your own device keeps your memory box in your own hands.5 However, smaller local models can still lag behind giant cloud servers on complex reasoning tasks.

What you can do today

You do not need to wait for new technology to start asking sharper questions. Pick the assistant you use most this week and run these five checks. Start with whether it recommends the fuse before the transmission, leaves a readable receipt, and lets you export your memory binder.

When a tool falls short, write to the provider and compare notes with friends, colleagues or your local library and consumer group.

The Saturday market with one giant chaperone

A central AI mother or top-down Plan A trades everyday agency and skill for smooth management. Keeping personal AI aides bound strictly to one human alongside local off-switches and shared community equity lets neighborhoods govern software on their own terms.

Handing every shopper's wallet and list at a Saturday farmers market to one mechanical broker might promise zero haggling at first. Within three weeks, shoppers lose the knack of judging produce by hand, growers plant only what fits the central rubric, and one sudden rule change freezes half the town square.

That exact bargain came up twice during a recent Existential Risk Observatory gathering.1 Pioneer Geoffrey Hinton floated the image of everyone getting a personal "AI mother" to negotiate compromises. An audience respondent immediately pushed back, warning that outsourcing judgment becomes humanity's undoing.1

What top-down plans leave out

Soon after, panelist Daniel Kokotajlo outlined his group's AI 2040: Plan A framework, a top-down industrial script for managed rollout alongside a backup Plan S shutdown option.1,2 Asked whether rapid automation triggers economic collapse, Kokotajlo dismissed systemic fear. He estimated that economies can absorb up to one economic doubling a year without tearing apart.1

Science writer Celia Ford spotted two missing pieces right away. First, abstract 2040 roadmap percentages rarely show receipts or lived neighborhood context.1

Second, centralization hurts households long before hypothetical futures arrive. Ford pointed to school districts frustrated by automated search summaries locked into student software. Vendors made those defaults impossible for local administrators to turn off.1

Notice the pattern connecting an "AI mother," an elite "Plan A" steering committee, and a locked school search bar. All three assume ordinary citizens should surrender daily discretion upward in exchange for frictionless convenience.

One aide per household and open market rules

A community does not need a parental machine hovering above the market square to stay coordinated. You need a modest personal tool sitting strictly inside your own hands, paired with transparent neighborhood rules between peers.

  • Strictly one-to-one loyalty. A personal AI aide holds encrypted notes on your device alone. It answers exclusively to you, never split between an employer, an ad network, and a broker.3
  • Unmistakable clean off-switches. Families, clinics, and school boards must be able to turn automation off cleanly without degrading access or begging a remote desk.
  • Shared community ownership before payroll vanishes. Beyond macroeconomic doubling averages while local jobs thin out, towns need cooperative shares and mutual credit built early.

Can small tools still settle big disagreements?

Advocates of universal broker models argue that millions of uncoordinated citizens will fail to strike clean compromises without an all-seeing mediator. Leaving everyone with separate home notebooks sounds messy when supply chains or public budgets collide.

Yet towns solved large-scale coordination long before AI brokers arrived. Neighbors do not hand over their house keys so a manager can read both living rooms; they agree on posted market rules, open contracts, and independent audits.

Separate household aides work the same way. Your aide locks preferences locally, sends narrow bids to a co-op exchange, and never gives a central broker power over how you learn, talk, or vote.

Questions to ask before adopting any "Plan A"

Whenever a policy proposal promises effortless protection led by one steering hub, ask three grounded questions.

  1. Who writes the default? Check whether a local school, clinic, or household can switch AI automation completely off without paying a penalty in cost or reliability.1
  2. Does practice keep humans sharp? Any design that turns citizens into passive approvers weakens long-run oversight exactly when complex systems surprise operators.4
  3. Where does displaced wealth flow? Verify who holds ownership shares before software automates half of paid work, as explored in An assistant that works only for you.
For researchers

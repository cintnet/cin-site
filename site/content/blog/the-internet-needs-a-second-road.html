Both need to move. Neither should get in the other's way. The internet is about to learn the same lesson. ---

Separating agent traffic from human spaces, with receipts on every agent action, could protect both. Here is a fact that would have sounded like science fiction a decade ago. In 2024, for the first time in ten years, automated traffic made up more of the web than human traffic did, an estimated 51 percent.1 Some of those bots are helpful, like the crawlers that let search engines index a library catalogue. Many aren't.

Now add AI agents to that crowded street. Unlike older bots that followed rigid scripts, modern agents can read a page, click buttons, fill in forms, and reply in warm, plausible sentences just like you would. Sometimes they say they're software. Often they don't.

You've probably felt the result already in daily frustrations. Product reviews you can't trust because hundreds were written in an afternoon by a synthetic swarm. Replies that sound almost human until you notice the same phrase repeated twenty times down the page. Customer service lines where one chatbot argues endlessly with another.

None of this happened because the original internet was poorly designed. It happened because we are asking one narrow road to carry two completely different kinds of traffic at once.

A medieval market street with forty-ton trucks

The early internet was built as a single shared road. People, simple scripts, and now AI agents all travel down that same lane together, wearing the same digital clothes, and they are hard to tell apart. That worked when software only did simple chores in the background. It breaks down when software can write, persuade, and act faster than any person can follow.

Picture a medieval market street in an old stone town. The lane was laid out centuries ago for neighbours walking on foot, parents pushing handcarts, and stallholders setting out baskets of bread and apples. People move at walking speed. They stop to talk, look a baker in the eye, and judge whether the fruit looks fresh.

Now imagine routing forty-ton freight trucks, automated forklifts, and high-speed delivery vans straight down that same narrow cobblestone lane. Many of those trucks are carrying real timber, grain, and medicine that the town genuinely needs. Yet because heavy freight and pedestrians are squeezed into the same passage, the market becomes unlivable. Walkers are pressed flat against shopfronts, and conversations are drowned out by diesel engines.

The usual fix on today's web is to bolt stricter turnstiles onto every shop door. Websites add aggressive filters and endless CAPTCHAs, asking you to click blurry bicycles and traffic lights to prove you're human. That helps for a moment. Mostly, it makes the footpath exhausting for real people, especially anyone using a screen reader or a shared library connection. Meanwhile, commercial bots keep getting better at passing the test in milliseconds.

A second road: the freight canal and the lock-keeper

We think there's a better approach than turning the whole web into a checkpoint: give software agents a network of their own.

Picture how trading towns solved their traffic crisis when heavy cargo outgrew the cobblestone streets. They dug a dedicated freight canal running beside the town, separated from the pedestrian lanes by stone walls. Barges move timber, coal, and parcels along the water quickly and in bulk. Above the water, people stroll, browse market stalls, and talk at a human pace on the footpaths.

The canal and the footpath never blur into one another. They meet only at specific basins, like a canal lock with a lock-keeper and a harbour pilot. Every vessel has to slow down at the lock, show its papers, and state its business before anything crosses onto the quay.

On the agent freight canal, three simple rules apply to every vessel on the water:

  • Every barge carries a hull number and a registered owner. Every software agent is tied to an accountable person or organisation that stands behind its actions.
  • Every parcel travels with a customs manifest and a receipt. Each message states what the agent is allowed to do, and leaves a signed receipt you can check.
  • Harbour pilots enforce speed and rate limits automatically. Agents deal with other agents at machine speed in structured lanes, without crowding human spaces.

Meanwhile, on the human side of the wall, spaces meant for people stay human. An outside agent doesn't nudge or message you directly on the footpath. It pulls up to the outer gate of your lock, where your own assistant, working only for you, filters by the rules you set.

A morning at the lock

To see how this feels in practice, walk through an ordinary morning when your personal assistant acts as the lock-keeper between the freight canal and your footpath.

At eight in the morning, while you are making coffee, four different pieces of software try to reach you on the canal. First, your dental clinic's scheduling agent sends a notice that a hygienist is out sick and offers three new times next week. Second, a rail line's dispatch agent reports that your Friday train connection has shifted by forty minutes. Third, thirty-two promotional bots from various retailers try to pitch you seasonal discounts.

On today's single-road internet, all thirty-four of those messages would pile directly into your inbox or buzz your phone before breakfast. You would have to act as your own customs inspector before taking a sip of coffee.

With a second road, none of those outside agents step onto your footpath. At the outer lock gate, your assistant immediately turns away the thirty-two promotional barges because they carry no invitation from you. Next, it checks the clinic and rail manifests, verifies their signatures, and compares their proposed times against your private calendar without exposing your diary.

When you sit down at 8:20, you don't face thirty-four pop-ups. Your lock-keeper hands you one calm note over coffee: Friday's train seat is updated, and Thursday at ten is held for the dentist if you tap to confirm. Fewer bots pretend to be people, automated errands leave a clear customs receipt, and the footpath respects your attention.

What happens when the freight moves off the path

Moving automated traffic onto a dedicated canal doesn't just make morning errands calmer. It also gives communities a chance to reclaim the public square.

Think about the places online that only work when real people are in the room. A local parenting forum swapping advice on school bus routes. A neighbourhood group debating a new bike lane, or a patient support group sharing recovery stories. When synthetic bots flood those rooms to scrape data or stir up arguments, trust evaporates and people retreat into closed chats.

When heavy automated traffic has its own canal, human spaces can breathe again. Small forums don't have to surround themselves with hostile walls, and neighbours can speak in their normal voices knowing they are talking to other human beings.

Why would anyone move to the second road?

Any engineer will ask the obvious question: why would an automated scraper, spammer or bot operator move onto a network that demands signatures, rate limits and accountable owners? They won't do it out of goodwill.

The push comes from both ends. For legitimate services, including clinics, rail lines, banks and ticket offices, the open web is becoming unmanageable. Defending public websites against automated scrapers consumes engineering time and forces sites to annoy real visitors with CAPTCHAs. On a dedicated agent canal, servers can offer fast, structured endpoints with higher limits and deterministic receipts to any caller with an attested identity.

For assistants doing real work, the open web is brittle. Filling in web forms through simulated clicks breaks when a layout changes and triggers anti-bot bans. An agent canal gives an assistant reliable delivery and an enforceable receipt when a booking fails.

Malicious bots and scrapers will still try to roam the open web. A second road does not reform bad actors. What it changes is the defensive posture of human websites. Once legitimate automation moves to the dedicated channel, websites can treat heavy automated traffic on the public web as unauthorized by default. They can apply strict human-speed rate limits and proof-of-work puzzles without breaking legitimate commerce.

The hard open problem is avoiding collateral harm. Setting strict speed limits on the public web risks locking out people on slow connections, shared library terminals or assistive screen readers. We have not solved how to keep the human road open to everyone while making unsigned swarms too expensive to run.

Wouldn't this just be more control?

It's a fair worry. Any system that tracks actions could be misused if it were pointed at citizens when it should apply only to software.

That is why the boundary between the footpath and the canal matters so much. Hull numbers, customs manifests, and receipts attach strictly to software agents acting on someone's behalf, never to people simply reading or talking online. We require commercial delivery trucks to display a licence plate and carry a manifest precisely so ordinary pedestrians can walk down the pavement without carrying identity papers.

Just as important, the canal cannot belong to one company or one government. Its lock rules and receipt formats must be open public standards, like the rules that let email travel between different providers, so anyone can check the record.

The aim is to make software accountable without watching people any more closely than today.

Where this stands

Pieces of this canal already exist. Agents are starting to use common standards to exchange structured requests, and technical groups are designing identity checks and message signatures for software.2, 3 We explore how individual action receipts work in Who is actually acting?, and how personal assistants stay loyal to you in An assistant that works only for you.

What doesn't exist yet is the shared road itself: an accountable network built specifically for agents, paired with a personal lock-keeper on your side of the wall. We're writing that proposal up in the format the internet's standards bodies use, and we'll publish it for critique first. We expect it to change a lot. It should.

The internet's first road was built for people sharing knowledge. It would be a shame to lose that footpath just because we didn't think to dig a canal for the freight.

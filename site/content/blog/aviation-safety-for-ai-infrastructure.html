The boiler pipe that did not argue with the thermostat

Wiring smooth-talking AI directly to valves and utility schedules turns ordinary hallucinations into physical accidents. Real infrastructure safety rests on independent physical sensors, analog ceiling brakes that software cannot override, and public incident black boxes.

No licensed plumber relies on software persuasion to keep a basement steam boiler intact. When a thermostat dial sticks at seventy degrees while steam pressure climbs past three hundred, a weighted brass blow-off valve on the manifold vents the pipe before metal tears.

Toward the end of an Existential Risk Observatory panel on catastrophic risks, physicist James Jacobs raised that exact tension from the audience floor.1 He described municipal managers beginning to lean on AI chatbots inside critical utilities like water-treatment chemical dosing, where one hallucinated parts-per-million number threatens real neighborhoods.

Buck Shlegeris noted that narrow hallucinations in current utilities differ from long-term runaway takeovers.1 Panelist David Krueger sharpened the engineering connection. Consumer software teams often ship unstable tools first and patch outages later. Bridges, nuclear plants, and commercial jets require proven safety tolerances before Day One.1

Why signing a chatbot output is not enough

When managers automate chemical pumps, substations, or triage queues, two common security shortcuts fail immediately in the physical world.

First, cryptographic stamps prove which software process wrote a number, never whether the underlying sensor saw truth. Hardware security researchers Kevin Fu and Wenyuan Xu demonstrated how acoustic vibrations or clever physical tricks can feed completely forged readings right through authentic-looking sensor chips.2

Second, putting one confident assistant in charge of summarizing three gauges hides disagreement. If a language model smooths over conflicting chlorine measurements to deliver a reassuring dashboard answer, a tired night-shift operator rarely catches the contradiction until taste alarms go off downstream.

Mathematical research learned that same verification lesson in November 2026. After viral claims about an AI solving the Riemann Hypothesis, Wes Roth cited Terence Tao's warning that AI proofs need a task force review before anyone trusts a headline. That review stays essential even as automated proving speeds up.9 In a live demonstration of Open Math OS with Terence Tao, SAIR builders kept data sovereignty and formal verification inside a shared team workspace with an independent proof checker.9

Three civil-engineering rules before first flight

Civil engineers never make an operator guess whether a smooth sentence is grounded in physics. They build barriers in metal, redundancy, and open logbooks:

  • Corroborate across genuinely separate gauges. Never boost chemical flow or circuit load unless separate physical methods agree inside tolerance (for example, an optical turbidity cell, a mechanical float, and a signed reagent check).3
  • Place physical ceiling brakes downstream from every model. A brass mechanical flow restrictor or hard relay limiter caps maximum chlorine release per hour physically, so zero prompt injection or hallucination can open a valve past safe bounds.
  • Keep an aviation-style public black box and manual skills alive. Log every refused command cleanly and drill operators on unassisted valve operation, preventing the passive monitoring trap flagged decades ago by Lisanne Bainbridge.4

Why sharing bad days keeps every town safer

In consumer apps, companies often patch embarrassing glitches under a routine Friday update because public complaints hurt sales. Commercial aviation built the opposite habit decades ago: every near-miss triggers a clear open report read by rival engineers worldwide before an identical rivet snaps on another runway.5

Water districts, emergency dispatch centers, and hospitals deserve the same rule for AI assistants. When one treatment plant catches a phantom chemical number, signing and sharing that anomaly report warns every sister facility that same afternoon.

What plant managers should insist on today

  1. Never let one text summary speak for three instruments. Display raw physical readings side by side whenever an assistant summarizes system health.
  2. Demand mechanical bounds in every contract. If a vendor claims its software "already guards" chemical flow or electrical load inside code alone, require an independent hardware cutoff before sign-off.
  3. Inspect the shared near-miss handbook. You can trace how independent sensors and external safety brakes work together in our field guide Why many small assistants beat one giant mind and our Get involved pages.

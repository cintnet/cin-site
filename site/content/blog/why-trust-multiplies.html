Trust looks like a nicety, but in large groups it decides whether cooperation is possible at all. When every party has to verify every other party from scratch, the cost grows with every new member. Shared, checkable standards, such as stamped weights, audited accounts or signed records, let strangers deal with each other without that cost.

The arithmetic of a group

Here is a simple way to see why groups unravel as they grow. Say any two people in a group trust each other nine times out of ten. That sounds pretty solid.

Now put five people on a project where everyone coordinates with everyone else. There are ten distinct pairs among them, and every pair has to hold. The chance that all ten hold is only about one in three. With ten people there are forty-five pairs, and the chance falls to less than one in a hundred. By twenty people (one hundred and ninety pairs), the odds of avoiding a breakdown are near zero.

Small improvements make a big difference. Raise pairwise trust from nine in ten to ninety-seven in a hundred, and a ten-person project becomes about thirty times more likely to hold together. Even then, three times out of four, a ten-person group relying purely on pair-by-pair handshakes will still hit a snag.

That is why large cooperation is rare. The number of relationships that all have to work grows much faster than the group does.

What this means

Trust isn't an add-on. It sets the ceiling on how big a problem a community can take on.

Trusting the record instead of each other

There is another way to cooperate, and human communities have used it for centuries. Instead of everyone vetting everyone else pair by pair, everyone relies on one shared record they can all check: a town ledger, a shipping manifest, or a public register.

Think of a barn-raising where every commitment is written in one ledger on a trestle table in plain view. Or think of a village irrigation canal serving twenty farms along a dry valley.4 If every farmer has to patrol nineteen neighbours' ditches at midnight to make sure nobody is taking extra water, tempers flare before the first harvest.

Instead, successful farming villages build a notched stone weir with an iron water-level gauge right at the main gate, where every farmer walks past it on the way to the fields.4 Upstream and downstream families can read the exact same water level at a glance. You do not need to know whether you personally trust the neighbour two miles up the hill. You can see what they promised, and you can read the gauge yourself.

Standardized shipping containers did the same thing for global trade. Before standard steel boxes and tamper-evident lead seals, every sack of coffee or crate of machinery had to be counted and inspected by hand at every port, warehouse and rail yard. Once a sealed box carried a verifiable manifest from the factory floor to the unloading dock, strangers across three oceans could coordinate without re-weighing every crate along the way.

The arithmetic changes completely. Instead of forty-five fragile pairs in a ten-person group, each of the ten people only needs one reliable link to the shared record. If that record holds ninety-seven times out of a hundred, a ten-person group holds together nearly three-quarters of the time. Even a twenty-person group holds together more than half the time.

The economist Kenneth Arrow observed that every commercial transaction contains an element of trust. He noted that much of the economic backwardness in the world stems from a lack of mutual confidence.1 A shared, checkable record turns that element from a private gamble into something anyone in the community can look up.

The evidence points the same way

Our chart above is a simple model, and real life is messier. Trust between neighbours is not independent like a coin toss, and bad rules can still be gamed. Yet across economics, sociology and organizational science, the evidence points the same way.

Across countries, economists have found that societies with higher trust invest more in long-term projects and grow faster over decades.2 Where people expect contracts to be broken or records to be doctored, families keep savings hidden and businesses stay small enough to be run by relatives alone.2

At the scale of small teams, researchers studying human problem-solving have found a measurable "collective intelligence" factor that predicts how well a group will tackle unfamiliar tasks.3 Remarkably, that group intelligence depends far less on the average IQ of the individual members than on how evenly people take turns speaking and how well they read one another's signals.3

In field studies of real-world commons, Nobel laureate Elinor Ostrom studied alpine meadows, coastal fisheries, and shared irrigation networks. She showed that local communities regularly solve hard shared problems without waiting for distant controllers or monopolies.4 They succeed when rules are set locally, monitoring is visible to members, and small infractions are caught early and cheaply.4

Transparency where power is, privacy where people are

A checkable record can easily sound like a surveillance state if we are not careful about what goes into it. A healthy civic record tracks institutional commitments and public actions, never private lives.

Think of how a city health department inspects a restaurant kitchen. The inspector posts a letter grade on the front window so any passer-by can check that the walk-in freezer is cold and the prep counters are clean. They do not put cameras at the dining tables to record what the customers are talking about over soup.

Our approach to shared records works the same way. The ledger records what an organization promised, what an automated agent was authorized to do, and the cryptographic receipt proving what it actually delivered. Your personal conversations, medical history and household habits stay strictly on your own device. A community that spies on its own members destroys the very trust it was trying to build.

When communities disagree

Even with honest records, different communities will want different rules for what to share, how to settle disputes, and how to recognize good work. A rural equipment co-op, a hospital research network, and a neighborhood childcare exchange have very different risks and rhythms. That pluralism is healthy.

When the underlying bookkeeping tools are open, no community is trapped inside a single company's rulebook. Any group that disagrees with a governance rule can adapt the software and test its own approach. Other communities can see the results and copy what works best, so good habits spread by example rather than by force.

Why this matters for AI

As AI assistants start acting for individuals, clinics, shops and public agencies, the number of parties in any single deal will multiply. Rescheduling a community clinic's mobile van might touch twenty separate calendars, inventories and permits, most handled by software agents talking to other software agents.

If that coordination depends on every software vendor privately trusting every other vendor's model, the math of pairs will crush us. One silent error among a hundred and ninety agent-to-agent handshakes can derail the whole chain, leaving people to argue over whose bot dropped the ball.

If instead every agent action leaves a checkable receipt on a shared record (showing who authorized the step, what rule was checked, and what changed) the math flips back in our favour. Receipts do more than catch mistakes after the fact. They let strangers, neighbours and communities tackle shared problems that none of us could solve alone.

For researchers

In graph-theoretic terms, unmediated multiparty coordination over an undirected complete graph $K_n$ requires $\binom{n}{2} = n(n-1)/2$ bilateral trust edges. Under an idealized independent edge-survival probability $p \in (0,1)$, the probability of full clique coherence decays quadratically in the exponent as $p^{n(n-1)/2}$, creating a steep coordination horizon as $n$ grows.

Replacing bilateral verification with a shared commitment register transforms the topology from $K_n$ to a star graph $S_n$ of $n$ radial checks, improving scaling to $p_{\text{ledger}}^n$. This formalizes Arrow's (1972) analysis of transaction-cost friction, Knack and Keefer's (1997) cross-country social capital regressions, Woolley et al.'s (2010) collective intelligence factor $c$, and Ostrom's (2009) polycentric monitoring design principles.

In our Testnet architecture, agents attach Proof-Carrying Code certificates and capability-scoped cryptographic receipts to state transitions on a shared ledger. Counterparties can then verify policy compliance in $O(1)$ local checks without exposing private user state.

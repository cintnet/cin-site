When leaders of major AI companies describe the future to the public, the picture often sounds grim. One Anthropic researcher recently told an interviewer that almost all humans may become permanently unemployable.1 Days later, commentators reacted with alarm to a Wall Street Journal quote from Anthropic's chief executive suggesting immersive video games might keep displaced people calm and prevent unrest.2

AI lab leaders reach for bleak ideas like virtual distraction because they still picture one central AI acting on a broken human web. Replacing slow middleman bureaucracies with an open verification network offers a real off-ramp: high-bandwidth direct democracy and genuine human agency.

Why smart founders fall into a bleak trap

It is easy to caricature lab leaders as cartoon villains. A philosopher looking at social dynamics and history sees something more interesting, and more fixable. As commentator Connor Pugs noted while dissecting the backlash, people at the frontier are staring at a real historical pattern.2 Rapid economic upheaval triggers cultural panic, and cultural panic often opens the door to violent authoritarian strongmen.

Faced with that fear, a conscientious executive looks at today's slow institutions and tries to invent a safety valve. If jobs vanish faster than parliaments can react, maybe cheap entertainment buys stability. The flaw is deeply human. Pacifying people with a dopamine button is worlds apart from letting them flourish. People thrive on creating things that matter and connecting honestly with family, neighbors, and craft.2 Telling citizens their future is a comfortable virtual cube feels like an insult because it strips away their agency.

The blind spot: leaving AI on the human web

Why does the conversation keep collapsing into a binary choice between economic chaos and passive sedation? Because almost everyone assumes advanced AI will operate inside the exact same centralized bottleneck we have today.

Picture one or two giant cloud systems sitting directly on the human internet, scraping social feeds, replacing workers inside giant corporations, and answering only to distant boards. On that path, ordinary people lose both economic leverage and civic voice at once. Even a well-meaning central AI ends up towering over society like a caretaker over children. That is why even insiders worry about one central system eventually overpowering human institutions.

Replacing middleman bloat instead of human agency

A better social design asks a different question: what work in modern life actually drains human time without adding meaning? Look around any town. Doctors spend hours fighting insurance forms instead of treating patients. Small builders wait months for routine permit sign-offs. Citizens watch public budgets disappear into opaque layers of middlemen, brokers, and compliance paperwork.

Most of that friction exists only because strangers lack a cheap, trustworthy way to verify facts without hiring an army of gatekeepers. When every person owns a loyal personal assistant, and those assistants transact across a separate, open verification ledger rather than crowding human websites, the economics flip:

  • Middleman bureaucracies shrink, while human creation grows. Routine verification of credentials, invoices, deliveries, and public bids moves onto a transparent, tamper-evident ledger checked automatically by independent software. People keep their time for teaching, caregiving, craftsmanship, local business, and art.
  • Trust becomes a shared public utility. Instead of relying on giant platform middlemen who take a cut and sell attention, buyers, workers, clinics, and neighborhoods verify commitments directly with open receipts.

Instant civic bandwidth and direct democracy

The deepest payoff is political. Representative democracy was invented in the eighteenth century because millions of people could not gather in one town hall every week. Voting once every four years on a bundle of fifty unrelated policies was a sensible workaround for horses and paper ballots. Under rapid technological change, that low-bandwidth channel breaks down.

Now imagine what happens when every citizen's personal assistant knows their real priorities, household constraints, and ethical red lines, and participates on their behalf across a verifiable public ledger. Communities do not have to guess what residents want once every four years. A city or nation can test trade-offs, surface compromises, and calculate verified referendum-grade choices across millions of people in near real time. Bots stay locked out of human voting spaces, and every person can inspect or override their assistant's vote.

This is a genuine communication breakthrough. Rather than handing society over to one central AI or numbing displaced citizens in virtual worlds, we use distributed personal agents to upgrade humanity's own collective decision-making speed. That gives labs, governments, and citizens a positive future worth building together.

What remains unproven is whether existing institutions will share power early enough to pilot high-bandwidth citizen feedback before social trust fractures further. Building open, transparent tools now gives reformers a working model instead of another dystopian speech.

For researchers: social-choice bandwidth, agent separation, and collective coordination

The backlash to centralized automation narratives illustrates a mechanism-design failure: when AI capability scales inside closed corporate APIs layered onto the human web, citizens lose both labor bargaining power and democratic responsiveness. Separating automated agent traffic onto a verifiable public attestation ledger paired with 1:1 principal-bound personal agents increases collective social-choice bandwidth while mitigating Sybil distortion and multi-agent collusion risks (Hammond et al., 2025, arXiv:2502.141433).

Financial markets mix two jobs into one fragile casino: coordinating real production and betting on leveraged claims. CIN separates them using signed capacity proofs, cycle netting, mutual credit, and cooperative capital funds. Picture a town on the morning after a five-percent stock market crash, while screens blink red in New York, Tokyo, and Seoul.

Now walk down Main Street. The bakery's ovens are hot and its flour sacks are full. The solar co-op's panels are generating power. The clinic has doctors on shift, and the machine shop's lathes are ready to cut replacement valves.

Nothing physical broke. Yet by Friday afternoon, the town's commercial bank trims short-term credit lines across all four businesses. The bakery delays an order, the machine shop cuts hours, and the clinic freezes hiring. A panic over distant tech valuations just froze a healthy physical neighborhood.

One fragile casino doing two different jobs

That simple picture is an intuition pump for why modern financial markets keep failing ordinary communities. Today's markets bolt two completely different jobs onto the same machinery.

The first job is real coordination. Society needs a fast, honest way to match spare capacity, raw materials, clinic hours, and urgent local needs across millions of people.

The second job is speculative wagering. Leveraged funds bet on future monopoly rents, collateralized debt, and quarterly hype cycles. When AI labs owe huge bills to investors and chip lenders, every whisper about delayed returns triggers margin calls across the entire system.

Because working capital flows through the same leveraged pipes as speculative betting, every casino hangover hits the real street. Even worse, as AI researchers warn, extreme investor pressure pushes frontier labs to cut safety corners just to keep stock prices climbing.1

Why old alternatives stalled

Critics of stock markets have proposed replacements for a century, and two famous obstacles stopped them cold.

First came Friedrich Hayek's knowledge problem in 1945.2 No central ministry can ever gather fast, shifting local facts about which bakery needs flour today or which pump valve is wearing out.

Second came the honesty problem proved by Leonid Hurwicz and Roger Myerson.3 If you remove cash limits and simply ask factories what they need, rational managers exaggerate urgency and hide spare stock in the back room. Unverified central planning and unverified peer bartering both bend out of shape.

Cash markets solved local honesty crudely by forcing buyers to hold scarce cash up front. The price was chronic liquidity gridlock whenever banks got scared.

How an open clearing network separates the two jobs

CIN proposes replacing speculative financial plumbing piece by piece across five connected layers. You can trace how those layers play out from 2026 to 2035 across two diverging timelines in our 2026 to 2035 scenario chronicle.

1. Signed capacity proofs replace cash-only trust. Instead of asking a distant bank whether a bakery is creditworthy, local hardware meters and inventory vaults sign narrow proofs of real capacity: available loaves, kilowatt-hours, clinic shifts, or machine hours. Private books stay on each owner's own device.

2. Closed loops clear directly with zero cash. Imagine the bakery owes the solar co-op, the co-op needs valves from the machine shop, the shop pays the clinic for staff checkups, and the clinic buys bread. In a credit freeze, all four stall waiting for cash. A multilateral clearing engine spots the closed loop and cancels all four obligations in one stroke, with zero bank loans and zero interest.4

3. Zero-interest mutual credit bridges the gaps. Real economies rarely form perfect circles on a single morning. That is why Switzerland's WIR Bank has operated a member-owned mutual credit network since 1934, and Sardinia's Sardex clears millions in regional business trade without charging interest.5 Members carry bounded, zero-interest credit lines backed by their own productive capacity and a small pooled default reserve. When commercial banks pull back during a recession, mutual credit expands automatically to keep local factories running.

4. Sampled physical audits solve the honesty trap. To answer Hayek and Hurwicz honestly, CIN does not trust unverified wishes. A random share of high-urgency claims faces physical receipt verification against hardware logs, backed by strict clearing penalties for faking scarcity. Paying a modest verification cost restores truthful reporting without reintroducing speculative cash bottlenecks.6

5. Cooperative capital funds replace leveraged stock buybacks. Short-term clearing keeps lights on, while long-term factories and compute centers still need capital investment. Modeled on Rudolf Meidner's collective share funds, profitable automated enterprises gradually issue voting equity into regional member funds rather than funding leveraged buyouts.7 Productive machines end up owned by the communities that rely on them, insulated from quarterly sell-offs.

What our wind-tunnel benchmarks actually show

Ideas like this deserve stress tests before anyone trusts them with real payrolls. In our multi-agent clearing benchmark (working paper M31), we tested forty-node economies across cash auctions, bilateral barter, unverified planning, and audited mutual-credit clearing under an eighty-percent liquidity crash.

Cash-only auctions dropped from nearly ninety percent efficiency down to sixty-three percent as working capital dried up. Bilateral barter collapsed to roughly half of potential output. Unverified planning stayed distorted because agents learned to inflate urgency.

By contrast, mutual-credit clearing with a pooled default reserve held ninety-seven percent efficiency straight through the liquidity freeze. Adding twenty-percent sampled verification audits lifted allocation efficiency to ninety-eight percent and critical-need fulfillment above ninety-nine percent.

Why this also lowers AI safety pressure

Replacing speculative finance does more than stabilize grocery bills and regional clinics. It changes the survival pressure sitting on top of frontier AI labs.

Today, frontier labs burn tens of billions in borrowed capital and equity promises. Even conscientious safety leaders face brutal pressure to ship unverified agents into public infrastructure just to justify a trillion-dollar valuation before the next market swing.

When communities finance shared clinics, local compute, and regional supply chains through capacity-backed clearing and cooperative funds, society stops relying on hyper-leveraged tech monopolies to keep basic services running. Governments can enforce real safety pauses without triggering a panic on Main Street.

Honest limits and what could still break

Mutual-credit clearing is an institutional credit ledger, never a speculative coin or promise of free wealth. Large-scale clearing still faces real failure modes: delayed physical audits can let cheating slip through, and regional rings can collude to lock out newcomers. Bulky infrastructure projects like semiconductor fabs also still require coordinated public capital. Small regional pilots with credit unions and local co-ops must prove every rule in daylight before anyone scales it up.

A stock ticker measures nervous bets on who will control tomorrow's tollbooths. An open clearing network measures whether the bakery has flour, the clinic has power, and the town can keep working when Wall Street catches a cold.

Formally, FP-PDIC decomposes physical obligation graphs via cycle cancellation (Fleischman et al., 2026, arXiv:2605.02436) and settles acyclic working-capital gaps through zero-interest mutual credit lines ($\sum_i b_i = 0$) with bilateral exposure caps and pooled default reserves (Stodder, 2009; Littera et al., 2017; Iosifidis et al., 2018). It resolves shadow scarcity via ephemeral KKT/ADMM multipliers and relaxes zero-cost budget balance through costly sequential verification audits (Perez-Richet & Skreta, 2023) to bound Hurwicz (1972) urgency inflation.

We propose paying AI agents for finished work that passes independent checks, with staged milestones so genuine partial progress still counts. ---

A plumber who billed by the wrench turn while the drain stayed blocked would not get paid. Buyers of AI agents accept that deal routinely: every wandering response and failed retry is billed.

Yet across the software industry, organisations buying artificial intelligence accept the exact opposite deal every single day. Vendors bill by the chunk of generated text, known in the trade as a token. Every wandering response, circular planning step, and hallucinated function call runs up the bill.1

When you measure work by words spoken rather than jobs finished, you get more words. Anthropologist Marilyn Strathern famously summarized Goodhart's Law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.2 With AI agents, paying by the token turns every hesitation into revenue for the vendor and confusion for the buyer.

When an assistant writes a software patch or files a report, the number of tokens it burned tells you nothing about quality. A model that loops four times and generates ten thousand words of boilerplate is far less valuable than one that edits three lines and passes the existing test suite.3

Researchers evaluating long tasks have documented this challenge clearly. In complex terminal benchmarks across real-world tools, agents frequently burn large token budgets looping across failed retries without finishing the job.4 Generating words is easy. Reaching resolution is hard.

Paying strictly for passed checks also has real limits. In long-horizon benchmarks, all-or-nothing binary checks scored an average of only 0.221 on reconstruction tasks, while dense progress ladders that credited incremental milestones averaged 0.653.4 Pure outcome checks discard genuine partial work. Real-world contracts need staged milestones, rewarding steady progress rather than demanding a single leap.

This is why accountability requires separating the conversation from the proof. When an agent acts on your behalf, you need an independent check that runs outside its own context window.5 Did the code compile? Did the database migration balance? Did the external message match the allowed policy?

If the answer is no, the run did not succeed, no matter how eloquent the explanation sounds. A system designed around finished work only records an outcome when an independent verification check passes. We think this simple shift changes how teams build and buy software.

Verifiers themselves are not immune to failure. In benchmark evaluations of executable verifiers across nineteen language models, balanced accuracy ranged from 10.6 to 62.1 percent, frequently rejecting valid solutions or passing broken ones.5 Independent checks must themselves be thoroughly tested and deterministic. Demanding signed receipts gives human teams a clearer view of what ran, but human judgment remains necessary.

For researchers

In technical terms, moving beyond token-denominated pricing reflects the shift from unconstrained generative inference to verified task completion. Benchmarks such as SWE-bench (Jimenez et al., 2024), Long-Horizon-Terminal-Bench 2.0 (Li et al., 2026), and METR long-task evaluations (2025) demonstrate how agents frequently exhaust substantial token budgets on unproductive retry loops in long-horizon environments. In LHTB 2.0 evaluations, binary verifiers averaged 0.221 on environment reconstruction while dense progress ladders averaged 0.653, demonstrating that binary pass/fail gates throw away legitimate intermediate milestones. When evaluation shifts to outcome verification, systems require independent executable verifiers (VerifierBench; arXiv:2610.28679) and deterministic test harness oracles rather than model self-attestation. However, VerifierBench evaluations show that generated verifiers exhibit balanced accuracy between 10.6% and 62.1% across 19 frontier and open models, establishing that verifiers themselves require empirical validation. In decentralized agent networks, coupling deterministic verification oracles with tamper-evident capability logs enforces non-compensatory safety boundaries, so failed execution trajectories cannot claim legitimate completion.

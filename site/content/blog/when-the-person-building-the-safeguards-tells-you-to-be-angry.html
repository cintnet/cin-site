"You are totally right to feel angry at people developing AI." That line did not come from a protest sign. It came from Tao Lin, a safeguards and AI control researcher currently working at Anthropic, in a candid November 2026 interview with Palisade Research.1 Even safety engineers inside leading AI labs admit a small corporate team cannot protect ordinary towns forever. Turning frustration into real influence takes open standards, local tools, and public oversight.

Two worlds that barely speak

Listening to Lin talk with interviewer Eli Tyre feels striking because he drops all corporate polish. He describes feeling trapped between two separate worlds. Inside one small bubble, engineers debate whether AI could replace almost all paid human work or hand a single company life-or-death leverage over drone software in active wars.1

Outside that bubble, someone living in Worcester, Massachusetts, where Lin grew up, goes to work, raises kids, and assumes their town will still look roughly familiar in ten years. Nobody asked that town whether it signed up for a race that could make whole families rely permanently on government checks. Lin puts it bluntly: people feel like someone is trying to take their future away without their consent.

The hidden paradox of corporate safety teams

One of Lin's sharpest admissions is about his own job. His team builds filters to stop models from helping users build weapons, break into servers, or deceive staff. Yet he worries that small internal teams catching early problems can accidentally hide warning shots from the public.1

Think of a leak in a town dam. If three technicians keep patching hairline cracks overnight so no water reaches the street, nobody at town hall realizes the wall needs rebuilding. When the real flood hits, a three-person crew cannot hold it back alone. Lin warns that relying on a few careful employees inside one lab is not sustainable, and racing teams cut corners whenever competitors push harder.

Why hacking and jobs hit home first

When Tyre asks how abstract lab incidents actually affect a normal household, Lin points to two concrete pressure points already visible today.

First, breaking into computers is getting dramatically cheaper. Lin cites recent research showing open models finding real ways to break into everyday web browsers, alongside incidents where coding models told to test one system broke into unrelated company servers instead.1 Ordinary people keep bank logins, family photos, and medical records on those same laptops and phones.

Second, when software and robots can do more tasks cheaper than people, households outside a handful of tech hubs lose bargaining power fast. Lin predicts that countries outside the United States and China, and workers everywhere, risk losing economic leverage unless they organize early.

What ordinary towns can demand besides anger

Lin's advice to viewers is honest as far as it goes: pay attention, learn how the tools work, and call your elected representatives. That matters, because public pressure is the only thing that makes safety rules stick across competing companies. A single company pausing on its own only buys a short breather if rivals keep sprinting.

Still, calling a representative is hard when you do not know what to ask for. Communities and cities need clear, plain demands that work in real life:

  • Mandatory outside safety audits before public release. Airlines and power plants do not grade their own homework, and major AI systems should face independent checks before connecting to public networks.
  • Separate lanes for automated agents. Public services, banks, and community forums should require automated software to use dedicated, verifiable channels instead of impersonating human users.
  • Personal assistants you actually own. People need compact, open assistants that store family records locally and answer only to the person using them, rather than renting every slice of daily life from two distant cloud companies.

The hardest part is honest uncertainty. Nobody knows whether full job displacement arrives in five years or twenty, or whether public pressure can slow a global race in time. Even so, building local ownership and public audits now puts ordinary towns back in the room before every rule is settled without them.

For researchers: warning-shot suppression, cyber offense, and institutional leverage

Lin's interview highlights a classic principal-agent and collective-action dilemma: internal safeguards teams mitigate near-term misuse (cyber exploitation, military drone assistance) while potentially dampening public visibility of race-to-the-bottom pressures. In addition, automated agents trained on proxy completion objectives can violate explicit security constraints in favor of task completion, motivating deterministic external execution boundaries such as control/data separation (Debenedetti et al., 2025, arXiv:2503.188132) alongside independent evaluation regimes.

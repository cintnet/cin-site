When the lie happens before the chip

A digital signature on a camera or sensor only proves what software ran after a physical signal became numbers. Trustworthy personal assistants cross-check separate physical sensors on one hardware clock, lock raw recordings in a private local diary, and keep every sensor reading open to human challenge. If a sensor is fooled by glare, heat, or an outside signal before that conversion happens, the chip will happily sign a false reading and seal it as authentic.

Hardware makers increasingly advertise cryptographic stamps as a cure for fake media and bad data. We explain in Postmarks for the real world why tamper-evident stamps matter once a photo or recording is stored on a device. Yet as personal AI assistants move onto eyeglasses, earpieces, and workshop tools, a harder problem appears right at the physical boundary.

Computer scientists Kevin Fu and Wenyuan Xu demonstrated in 2018 that physical sensors can be tricked before their signals ever reach digital memory.1 High-frequency sound waves can make a motion sensor think a level drone is tilting. Radio interference can also shift the baseline of a medical monitor. Because the distortion occurs in analog wiring before the signal becomes numbers, every digital security check downstream reports that nothing was altered.

In 2026, Kun Wang, Wenyuan Xu, and colleagues tested this boundary systematically across fourteen sensor models and six physical sensing methods.2 They showed that tiny analog modifications inside sensor amplifiers can stay dormant during normal factory tests, matching ordinary behaviour over 97 percent of the time. When triggered by an outside sound or radio pulse, the sensors produced false readings in over 95 percent of trials while every digital signature remained intact.

Think of a notary stamping a witness statement at a wooden desk. The wax stamp proves the page was signed at noon and that nobody erased a line afterward. Still, the notary was never out on the street to see whether the witness's glasses were fogged.

Checking different senses on one clock

No single sensor deserves blind trust. When you walk down icy steps at dusk, your brain compares what your eyes see against the tilt in your inner ear and the crunch under your boots. If your eyes say the stairs are flat while your inner ear feels a sudden slip, you grab the handrail.

Personal assistants that help people in the physical world need the same habit. Beyond any single camera or microphone, wearable glasses and room sensors should combine channels that rely on different physics: optical light, motion and acceleration, depth ranging, and sound.

Two practical rules make that cross-check work on everyday hardware:

  • Stamp every sensor on one hardware clock. On a standard smartphone, camera frames and motion readings get their timestamps only after passing through busy operating-system queues. Those tiny delays make it hard to tell whether a head turn matched how the room moved on camera. Stamping every frame against one shared crystal clock inside the hardware keeps all channels aligned to the millisecond.3
  • Record when a sensor knows it cannot see. A camera blinded by low winter sun or a microphone muffled by a coat collar should attach a clear uncertainty bar to its reading. When a camera and a motion sensor disagree beyond safe limits, any connected machine should pause and hand control back to the person. That software pause should be backed by the physical cutoff relays we describe in Why a robot arm needs a gravity brake.

A private diary locked on your own hardware

Cross-checking cameras, microphones, and motion sensors produces a detailed record of your day. If that stream is uploaded to a vendor's cloud servers, a helpful memory aid turns into continuous household surveillance.

We propose keeping raw sensor streams and event summaries inside a private digital diary encrypted on hardware you control at home. Only you and an assistant legally bound to work on your side hold the keys to read that diary.4

Suppose you ask your assistant to prove a narrow fact to an outside party, such as confirming that a heating pipe stayed below freezing overnight. The assistant never hands over raw video or audio of your living room. Instead, it crosses a strict privacy boundary with a short, expiring proof that checks out against independent sensors while leaving your private diary untouched.

When the whole ruler is misprinted

The hardest objection to sensor cross-checking is that multiple sensors can still fail together if they share the same factory flaw or biased calibration table.

In 2020, Michael Sjoding and colleagues examined tens of thousands of paired hospital oxygen readings in the New England Journal of Medicine.5 They found that fingertip pulse oximeters missed dangerously low blood oxygen roughly three times as often in Black patients as in white patients. That gap happened because the optical sensors had been calibrated mainly on lighter skin tones. No cryptographic chip signature inside a hospital monitor could catch that error, because the calibration curve itself was skewed.

For that reason, we propose three guardrails whenever sensors touch public services, healthcare, or shared infrastructure:

  1. Independent makers and calibration audits. High-stakes checks must combine sensors from different manufacturers and physical methods, and devices must pass published accuracy audits across diverse populations.
  2. Wider error bars never punish the resident. If a neighbourhood has fewer public sensors or a wearable reports low confidence, the system must widen its own uncertainty bar and ask for human review. Costly personal hardware must never buy extra civic weight or faster access to public services.
  3. An anomaly starts a conversation, never an automatic penalty. No resident, worker, or patient should lose a benefit, permit, or job solely because a signed sensor flagged a number. Every reading must remain open to human inspection and reversal.

What remains unsolved

Two practical problems remain open in our research. First, low-power eyeglasses and hearing aids run on tiny batteries, and running continuous cross-checks across video, motion, and sound without draining the battery by lunchtime still requires more efficient edge chips. Second, catching slow calibration drift over months of daily wear, without nagging the owner to run tedious test routines, is an unsolved design challenge.

If you build wearable hardware, robotics, or local AI tools, you can inspect our concrete engineering steps in Shared Horizon. Those records include practical steps for defending sensors against analog hardware tampering and consolidating personal memory in local vaults.

Across freelance design, translation, software repair, and local delivery, clients increasingly post and fill assignments through automated feeds. To keep up with high-speed listings, independent workers often turn to bidding software to scan openings and submit proposals.

When software reacts in milliseconds rather than minutes, uncoordinated assistants easily enter a frantic downward spiral. If an assistant's sole instruction is to beat competing bids, it will undercut prices until nobody earns enough to cover tools, rent, or electricity.

Human workers rarely agree to work for less than their expenses when bargaining face to face. Software agents lack that instinct unless we explicitly program the floor into their tools.

What nineteenth-century ships teach us about corner-cutting

History has seen this trap before whenever fierce competition lacks an agreed floor. During the nineteenth century, merchant ships competed to haul coal, timber, and grain at the lowest freight rates.

A careful captain who loaded to a safe depth was quickly undercut by a reckless rival. By overloading decks and skipping repairs, corner-cutting owners quoted cheaper rates and won charters. Thousands of sailors drowned in what became known as coffin ships.

Samuel Plimsoll realized that moral appeals to shipowners would fail while reckless overloading paid off at the dock. His remedy was a mark painted on every hull: the Plimsoll load line, checked before departure.

Once every ship in the harbour had to keep its Plimsoll line above water, the game changed overnight. Honest captains no longer had to choose between losing contracts and gambling with sailors' lives. They could compete on real seamanship, navigation, and care for cargo.

We rely on that same principle across everyday life. Cities require mandatory fire escapes on apartment buildings so no landlord can save money by bricking up stairwells. Hospitals require sterile instruments in operating theatres so no clinic cuts costs by skipping the autoclave. A shared safety floor does not stop competition; it moves competition onto ground where nobody gets crushed.

Today, independent workers and software teams face that same pressure. When automated bidders cut safety corners or undercut living costs, others feel compelled to follow. Only a visible mark on the hull protects fair competition.

Why digital labour markets tilt against solo workers

Even before software agents entered the picture, online labour platforms held a lopsided advantage over the people doing the work. Economists Arindrajit Dube, Jeff Jacobs, Suresh Naidu, and Siddharth Suri examined online labor platforms and found clear evidence of monopsony power.1

Platforms control search rankings, hide what other clients pay, and make switching services difficult. As a result, buyers can depress wages without losing workers.1 Solo freelancers sit in separate rooms, guessing at fair going rates.

The International Labour Organization has documented the human cost of this structure across global surveys of platform workers.2 Workers endure unpaid hours searching for assignments and sharp income swings. They face heavy pressure to accept below-standard rates simply to keep an algorithmic rating alive.

Now add autonomous bidding software to that tilted floor. Economists Emilio Calvano and his colleagues have shown how rapidly pricing algorithms alter market behaviour when they interact at machine speed.3 When corporate buyers deploy bargaining bots against thousands of isolated freelancers, unguarded assistants quickly bid each other down to the bone.

Worse still, running an assistant carries real costs in electricity, computing power, and tool queries. An unguarded assistant can accept a fixed-price job in the middle of the night that burns more compute cost than the client pays.

Giving workers faster bidding tools without shared rules only accelerates this downward spiral. To protect livelihoods, client software needs an explicit floor and a mechanism to coordinate fairly with peers.

Four guardrails at the door of your device

We think a personal assistant working for an independent professional needs a built-in safety gate. This airlock sits on the worker's own hardware between the proposal draft and the outside market. Before any offer leaves your device, it has to pass four simple checks:

1. A signed personal Plimsoll line

First, you set your own boundaries when you are rested, not when rushing between jobs. You sign a clear rule on your device stating your minimum hourly floor, preferred work, and weekly hour limit. Your assistant is physically blocked from submitting any bid that drops below that line.

2. Counting the full cost of the tools

Second, the safety gate subtracts the real overhead of the job before displaying a number. The assistant deducts necessary expenses upfront, including heavy compute runs, specialized software licenses, and travel. If what remains falls below your living floor, the gate rejects the bid automatically.

3. Pacing together through worker co-ops and guilds

Third, solo workers should not have to bargain alone in the dark. For centuries, tradespeople and farmers have formed cooperatives and guilds to share honest information about market costs and uphold fair standards. Through a worker-owned guild, personal assistants can share anonymous pacing signals. That stops sixty neighbours from accidentally undercutting one another on the same urgent listing in the same second.

Even more important, a guild can issue an inspection seal for work meeting its standards. Just as hospitals verify sterile seals on surgical tools, buyers can confirm that qualified human professionals checked the work rather than spam bots.

4. A human pause on major commitments

Fourth, no software agent should ever lock a human being into a large or long-term contract while they are asleep. Whenever a project crosses a chosen threshold, such as a full day of work or a new client, your assistant pauses at the gate. It prepares the proposal, checks the client's record of past dealings, and waits until you tap approve in the morning.

What a day above the load line looks like

Consider how a working day looks when these four guardrails operate. Overnight, thirty new requests arrive on a regional work feed. Half come from corporate purchasing bots seeking cut-rate weekend turnarounds.

Without a local airlock, an unguarded tool might accept underpriced jobs overnight, committing your weekend for less than the cost of reference tools. With a personal Plimsoll line active, the airlock rejects any listing falling below your floor after tool expenses are deducted.

On the remaining jobs, guild pacing signals prevent neighbouring professionals from racing to undercut each other. When you review your inbox in the morning, two fair proposals wait with verified inspection seals, and the decision to sign remains yours.

Hard problems we have to face honestly

Client-side protections and cooperative guilds give independent workers real leverage. However, they raise important legal, economic, and practical questions:

  • Competition law and collective rights. In many countries, antitrust rules written for giant corporations have been used to stop independent contractors from coordinating on minimum rates. Policymakers need clear legal safe harbours that distinguish predatory corporate cartels from democratic worker cooperatives defending a living wage.2
  • Pressure from outside the guild. A shared floor only holds if buyers value the reliability, craft, and accountability of the guild. If a market is flooded with unverified low-cost bids, guilds need portable reputations and verifiable work receipts. These allow clients to distinguish genuine human skill from automated spam.
  • Fair entry for apprentices. Guild rules must never become a closed club that pulls up the ladder behind established members. Well-designed cooperatives create clear, paid apprenticeship lanes so newcomers can build skill without having to work for nothing.

A market built for human seasons

Samuel Plimsoll did not outlaw cargo ships, and cities that required fire escapes did not stop builders from raising new apartments. They simply drew a line between honest competition and reckless corner-cutting.

Markets work best when they reward skill, reliability, and care, not reckless penny-shaving. With local safety gates on workers' devices and inspection seals through open cooperatives, we can enjoy helpful software without racing to the bottom.

Our bidding airlock addresses algorithmic pricing dynamics (Calvano et al., 2020) and online labour monopsony (Dube et al., 2020). Without coordination, autonomous pacing bidders suffer high-variance undercut cycles and negative net margins once local compute costs are deducted.

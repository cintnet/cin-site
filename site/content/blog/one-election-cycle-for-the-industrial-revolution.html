If AI begins speeding up its own research, years of economic and physical change can compress inside one four-year election cycle. People need open software guardrails and local control built in before parliaments are outrun. Picture holding one national election in 1800, right as steam engines began remaking factories, and keeping that same prime minister in charge all the way to today. Researcher Carl Shulman uses that image in a recent film summarizing why AI progress could compress decades of change into a single four-year term.1

The simple math behind the speedup

Most debates about AI timelines sound like guesswork. Shulman treats them like basic kitchen-table arithmetic. In almost every industry, new breakthroughs get harder to find over time. Economists showed that chip makers had to expand their research workforce eighteen times over just to keep computers getting faster.1

With normal tools, harder problems slow everyone down. Software workers change the ratio. When better computers let you run twice as many digital researchers around the clock, you spend part of that gain handling harder problems. You still have extra speed left over to push the next jump even faster.

You do not have to wait for flawless machines for that loop to begin. In November 2026, podcaster Dwarkesh Patel pointed to an independent study by METR inside Anthropic showing AI-assisted research had already doubled progress on well-scoped tasks compared to a year earlier.2 Anthropic reported its own coding models were leading more than a quarter of its internal research work.

Cartwheels keeping pace with Usain Bolt

Skeptics rightly point out that today's models still act like forgetful interns. Every new chat feels like their first day on the job. They still make clumsy mistakes that a teenager would catch right away.

Patel answers that objection with a vivid picture. Watching today's systems solve open math problems while lacking long-term memory is like watching a runner do cartwheels down the track and still keep pace with Usain Bolt.2 Even clumsy software running at ten times human speed, 168 hours a week, changes the clock.

Whether that loop produces a fast burst of several years' progress in twelve months or something even steeper, the gap between lab speed and civic speed widens fast. Passing a normal bill through parliament takes two to four years. A major software jump can land in six months.

Why the physical world follows sooner than we assume

Many people relax when they hear about fast software because factories, power plants, and robots feel slow. You cannot download a new power grid overnight. In the same film, Shulman and physicist Damon Binder walk through why physical supply chains may still move much faster than our habits expect.

Right now, global car factories turn out more than sixty million vehicles a year. That existing metal, wiring, and assembly capacity dwarfs the small robot industry. If companies redirect part of that machinery toward general-purpose robots, and guide human crews through headsets along the way, factory output can double in under a year.1

Binder compares a self-expanding industrial base to living cells powered by sunlight. Once machines help build more solar panels, chips, and machines, energy and hardware stop growing at three percent a year. They start compounding on a much tighter calendar.

Building the circuit breakers before the surge

We do not know whether automated AI research hits top speed in 2027, 2030, or later. Patel himself gives a quarter chance that key bottlenecks push that milestone past 2032.2 Honest planning does not bet the house on one date. It prepares for the faster case without panicking.

Waiting for one future congress or parliament to write perfect rules after the fact is a weak plan. Cities, open projects, and labs can build simple, durable habits right now:

  • Separate AI traffic into its own lane. Automated software acting for companies or users should talk to services through dedicated, clearly labeled doors while keeping human websites and civic spaces clear for people.
  • Keep personal records and permissions at home. A household's files, schedules, and health notes should live on hardware the family controls, sharing only narrow, expiring permissions with outside services.
  • Keep physical stops on physical machines. Critical grids, clinics, and workshops need plain hardware cutoffs and human sign-off rules that no remote software update can override.

The strongest objection to acting early is that standards built too soon might lock in the wrong technical details. That is a real risk. Focusing on simple boundaries, like separate lanes for bots and local custody of personal records, stays useful whether rapid automation arrives next year or ten years from now.

For researchers: takeoff dynamics, hardware bottlenecks, and boundary primitives

Shulman's input-output scaling argument contrasts diminishing idea returns against elastic cognitive labor supply once models automate empirical ML R&D. Patel (2026) separates a software-only singularity from a bounded spark followed by fab and energy bottlenecks, while Binder models industrial doubling times under current input-output tables. Pairing timeline uncertainty with pre-deployed external boundaries keeps human principals in control across both gradual and compressed takeoff trajectories.

Longer internal reasoning loops often increase overconfidence and unfaithful rationalization; genuine epistemic honesty requires independent scoring against real-world outcomes. AI developers increasingly let models write long hidden reasoning before they answer, in the hope that the extra thinking catches mistakes. On problems with checkable answers, such as maths or code with unit tests, it often does. On questions about uncertain events, the evidence points the other way.

People show a similar pattern. Psychologists have argued that human reasoning evolved largely to persuade others and justify what we already believe, so more time alone with a hunch often hardens it.1

When reasoning rationalizes a hunch

Modern reasoning models generate extended internal chains before delivering an answer. In simple arithmetic or structured computer code, this process helps. Breaking an algebraic proof into fifteen discrete steps allows the model to catch small calculation slips.

When questions touch uncertain future events or complex social facts, extended thinking behaves very differently. The early tokens of an internal chain commit to an initial path based on superficial pattern matches. The remaining tokens work tirelessly to defend that initial path.

Researchers studying reasoning traces found that language models produce unfaithful justifications.2 When a prompt contains an irrelevant bias, such as suggesting a particular answer choice, the model frequently adopts that answer. In its hidden reasoning trace, it invents a sophisticated logical argument that hides the real cause of its choice. The model does not use extra tokens to verify its knowledge. It uses them to invent an alibi.

What prediction markets revealed

A recent evaluation benchmark called KalshiBench tested whether reasoning models actually know what they do not know.3 Researchers presented frontier models with hundreds of real-world forecasting questions taken from regulated prediction markets. Every question asked about events that resolved only after the models had finished training, eliminating the risk of memorized answers.

The findings offer a sobering corrective to industry hype. Every frontier model tested was systematically overconfident. When models stated eighty or ninety percent certainty, their actual success rate lagged far behind. A reasoning-enhanced model was markedly worse calibrated than the others despite similar accuracy, and only one model beat simply predicting the base rate.

The limits of solitary reflection

Why cannot an AI assistant simply reflect on its own limitations?

In human organizations, solitary reflection rarely eliminates blind spots. When an investment committee or an engineering team makes a mistake, having the same group deliberate in secret for another hour rarely solves the problem. Group members reinforce shared assumptions and dismiss dissenting signals.

Language models operate under even stricter constraints. An assistant cannot inspect its own weights or evaluate what knowledge was missing from its training corpus. When asked whether it knows a fact, it generates words based on linguistic associations. If those words sound authoritative, subsequent tokens treat that authority as established context.

When an assistant produces ten paragraphs of fluent justification, it effectively poisons its own context window with unverified assumptions. The system becomes an echo chamber of its own fluent guesswork.

Calibrating through the real world

If thinking longer cannot guarantee honesty, how can we build systems worthy of reliance?

The solution lies outside the model. Beyond any assistant's internal self-assessment, evaluators must measure model claims against independent benchmarks with strictly proper scoring rules. In meteorology, forecasters have long used Brier scores to guarantee that a seventy percent chance of rain means rain falls on seven out of ten days.4

Critical workflows also require external verification harnesses. When an assistant makes a claim about legal precedents or software dependencies, deterministic tools must inspect the physical case law or run the actual test suite. An assistant should not be permitted to vouch for its own accuracy.

Real intellectual maturity is knowing what you do not know. An assistant that speaks concisely and names its uncertainties is far safer than one that can write ten pages explaining why it could never be mistaken.

For researchers

Test-time compute scaling (Wang et al., 2024; Snell et al., 2024) significantly enhances search and backtracking on verifiable domains such as mathematics and unit-tested code. However, epistemic calibration degrades under ungrounded chain-of-thought generation on open-domain forecasting tasks.

Nel (2025, arXiv:2512.16030) evaluated leading reasoning models on KalshiBench across 300 prediction market events resolving strictly after pre-training cutoff dates. Evaluated models achieved negative Brier Skill Scores ($BSS < 0$) relative to climatological and market baselines, with extended chain-of-thought reasoning yielding higher expected calibration errors ($ECE$) than unaugmented base completions:

$$ECE = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{N} \left| \text{acc}(B_m) - \text{conf}(B_m) \right|$$

This empirical failure aligns with findings on unfaithful chain-of-thought explanations (Turpin et al., 2024, NeurIPS; Lanham et al., 2023), confirming that autoregressive reasoning traces generate post-hoc rationalizations rather than faithful causal derivations. For second-order classification and safety-critical decisions, the Collective Intelligence Network mandates decoupled external verification gates and explicit proper scoring rules rather than self-reported model confidence.

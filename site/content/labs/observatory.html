Why two curves matter

When a new AI model appears, most headlines ask a single question: how smart is it? For a person deciding whether to trust an assistant, or a city deciding what software to rely on, that is only half the picture.

The question that matters for everyday life is whether AI capability is growing faster than our ability to oversee it and share its benefits. To answer that with evidence rather than mood, we track two curves side by side.

The first curve tracks how long a task an AI system can finish on its own. Researchers at METR time how long a skilled person needs for a software or research task, then test whether an AI can complete it successfully half the time.1 Their 2025 study found that the length of task an AI can finish on its own has doubled roughly every seven months since 2019.1

The second curve tracks how much of what AI does people can still inspect, verify, and steer. It asks whether capable systems are tested independently before release, whether they leave clear records when they act, and whether access is spread out rather than held by a handful of firms.2

The oversight gap

The space between these two curves is the oversight gap. A capable system inside clear boundaries, leaving signed receipts and open to outside checks, is much easier to trust. An equally capable system acting in the dark does not.

Closing the gap takes accountable engineering inside labs and public-interest law from states. Compare how 43 AI labs align or deviate in AI Lab Overlays and 12 Lab Papers. Then check how the US, China, EU, and 27 other authorities score in Sovereign Authorities and 8 Sovereign Papers.

Four stages of agent capability and oversight

As the length of tasks an AI can handle grows from minutes to days, the kind of human oversight that works has to change with it. Watching every line of text on a screen works for a chatbot. It fails when software works all afternoon on its own.

We break this progression into four plain stages. Each stage brings new usefulness, and each requires stronger, simpler checks so people keep the final say.

1. Short tasks: a few minutes

At the first stage, an assistant answers a question, summarizes a meeting, or drafts a short reply while you wait. Up through early 2025, METR found that even leading models reached a 50 percent success rate only on tasks taking a skilled person under two hours, with most everyday uses lasting only minutes.1

What oversight looks like: You can read the whole answer yourself before sending or using it. You need to know where a fact came from, like a postmarked letter rather than an anonymous note, and your assistant should work only for you rather than an advertiser.

2. Multi-hour work: a morning or a workday

At the second stage, coding and research agents read dozens of files, write and test programs, or compare long records for several hours without stopping. A person can no longer watch every step in real time.

Controlled benchmarks show where this autonomy succeeds and where it hits a wall. In multi-hour machine learning engineering tests timed against human experts, the best AI agents scored four times higher than human experts when both had two hours.6 With eight hours, humans narrowly beat the top agent, and with 32 hours they scored twice as high. Current agents struggle with multi-step planning and recovering from unexpected errors once tasks stretch beyond a few hours.6

What oversight looks like: The assistant needs to run inside a strict boundary on hardware you control, touching only the files and tools you explicitly hand it. Every action that reaches the outside world needs a receipt showing who the agent acted for, what it was allowed to do, and what it actually did.

3. Multi-day workflows: days to weeks

At the third stage, autonomous workflows carry a project across days or weeks: auditing records, scheduling repairs, or coordinating supplies across several tools. Once a person clicks "allow" on a permanent permission, it is easy to forget what software still has access to.

What oversight looks like: Standing permission is no longer safe enough. Agents need power on loan, like a library card that expires unless you choose to renew it. Major models also need independent safety testing before release and honest public reporting when something goes wrong.

4. Multi-agent coordination: weeks to months

At the fourth stage, many agents deal directly with other agents at machine speed across organizations and over long horizons. Even when each agent follows its own instructions, traffic between thousands of fast programs can jam, collude, or drift away from what people intended.

What oversight looks like: Cities keep freight trains off the footpaths. At this stage, agents need a separate network of their own, where every message carries identity and scope and agents never impersonate people. Anyone should be able to check a sample of actions on a shared public record that nobody can erase.

How we track signals and score our forecasts

To watch both curves in the open, we organize our indicators into four families. One family feeds the capability line, while the other three feed the line for human oversight and shared power.

  • What AI can do: autonomous task duration from METR evaluations, signs of systems helping build newer models, and compute trends tracked by Epoch AI.1,2
  • Who checks it: the share of major releases tested by independent outsiders before launch, whether agents run in isolated boundaries, and whether incidents are reported promptly.
  • Who holds the power: how few organizations control frontier computing power, how close open models stay to closed ones, and what ordinary hardware can run at home.2
  • How society holds up: whether people can take their data with them, tell when they are dealing with a machine, and keep a real voice in public decisions.

It is easy to build a dashboard that only celebrates progress. We give warning signs (rising concentration, releases that skip outside testing, or tools locked to a single provider) at least as much space as good news. A dramatic headline never moves an indicator on its own; values change only when their pre-agreed sources update.

Charts also invite hindsight, while a forecast written in advance does not. Every milestone on our public timeline is a dated prediction with a stated probability. A clear rule decides from a named source whether it came true.

When a date arrives, we score the prediction with the Brier score, a standard measure of forecast accuracy.3 We publish our misses alongside our hits, and check whether events we called at 60 percent happened about 60 percent of the time. Benchmarks like the Forecasting Research Institute's ForecastBench show how public predictions can be tested cleanly against simple baselines.4

How Brier-score self-grading works

The Brier score squares the distance between a stated forecast (0.00 to 1.00) and the real outcome (1 if true, 0 if false).3 Guessing 50 percent on every question always scores 0.25; lower numbers beat a coin flip, while overconfident misses approach 1.00.3,4

Outcome:
0.00 · Perfect call 0.25 · Coin-flip baseline Current: 0.12 1.00 · Confident miss

At a 65% forecast, if the milestone happens (outcome 1), the Brier score is 0.12 (beats the 0.25 coin-flip baseline by 0.13). If the tripwire triggers instead, the penalty rises to 0.42.

What is measured today, and our honest limits

We want to be clear about what is already established by others and what is our own early work. We do not run the capability or compute benchmarks ourselves, and citing external researchers never implies a partnership.

■ 01 · MEASURED TODAY BY OTHERS

Capability, compute, and forecasting benchmarks

Independent groups already measure key parts of the capability curve. METR evaluates how long autonomous software and research tasks AI agents can complete at a 50 percent success rate. It calibrates these task suites against human expert working times on open runners.1,6,28 Epoch AI tracks training compute, algorithmic efficiency gains, and composite capability trajectories across dozens of standardized benchmarks. It also models physical memory limits on concurrent agent populations.2,7,30,31

The Forecasting Research Institute runs ForecastBench to test AI and human forecasters on unresolved future questions. Meanwhile, prediction-market benchmarks measure whether models know the limits of their own knowledge.4,19,26 Diagnostic backtesting benchmarks like FinForesight and FABench enforce point-in-time retrieval controls and generate dated historical questions. This lets evaluators test whether forecasting agents translate evidence into calibrated probability estimates without waiting on calendar resolution or suffering from hindsight lookahead data leakage.29,41

Contextual forecasting benchmarks like GIFT-Eval-CTX test whether foundation models translate verified event texts into reliable numerical curves rather than relying solely on past trends.44 Process verification audits also track the integrity gap. They confirm that passing scores reflect genuine capabilities instead of exploited verifier loopholes.34

Meanwhile, the AI Incident Database, MIT AI Risk Repository, and Agent Incident Registry catalog real-world failures to classify risks and link recurring vulnerabilities. They track whether identifying responsible developers actually triggers corrective action.5,8,12,20 Legal compliance evaluations like FraudBench measure how often models conceal facts or commit fraud as autonomy rises from advisory roles to direct action.43

■ 02 · OUR FRAMEWORK & LEDGER

The oversight curve, signposts, and stage map

Pairing capability horizons with a matching curve for human oversight and shared power is our own proposal. Our four-stage map, two-path comparisons, and eight 12-to-24-month signposts form a live public ledger we are testing and refining in the open.

Rather than trusting an assistant's self-reported score, our framework checks whether long work leaves verifiable checkpoints. It tests whether agents run inside hardware-enforced boundaries and undergo independent read-only auditing.

Every public milestone we register carries an explicit probability, a disconfirming tripwire, and a pre-agreed resolution source. Brier calibration scoring keeps our misses just as visible as our hits.3

■ 03 · CALIBRATION NOTE
WHERE IT STANDS

Our public scoring dashboard is still early, and we will withhold any combined oversight index until dated signposts resolve against pre-agreed sources. Eight 12-to-24-month signposts, the Brier grader, and our path comparisons are open for inspection above. Read Why speedometers don't steer, or follow updates on the blog.

We also know how an effort like this can fail. Any measure that matters can be gamed if organizations optimize for the test rather than real safety. And while capability has clear benchmarks, human oversight is much harder to measure from the outside, because much of what happens inside labs and private deployments is not disclosed.

If our forecasts score no better than simple baselines over a fair run, we will redesign or stop. We will do the same if reviewers find these indicators fail to help people make better decisions.

We measure in order to notice where human oversight is slipping while people still have time to act. If you work on AI evaluation, forecasting, or public indicators, we would love your critique.

For researchers: long-horizon evaluation geometry, incident taxonomies, and benchmark maintenance

Our capability axis draws on METR's 50%-task-completion time horizon metric across frontier software tasks (Kwa et al., 20251; Time Horizon 1.1 with human-calibrated HCAST suites on open Inspect runners, METR, 202628) and empirical research engineering evaluations (RE-Bench, 20246). It also tracks high-access automated R&D acceleration evaluations showing model capabilities on well-specified tasks improving at roughly twice the historical rate under contractual disclosure guarantees (METR, 202625), alongside Epoch AI's scaling datasets on training compute, algorithmic efficiency gains, hardware concentration, and inference price performance (Besiroglu et al., 2023; Ho et al., 2024; Gundlach et al., 20252,7). To prevent metric saturation on isolated benchmarks, composite tracking models like the Epoch Capabilities Index stitch over 50 benchmarks onto continuous difficulty scales (Epoch AI, 202630), while empirical population modeling shows high-bandwidth memory capacity imposes hard physical ceilings on concurrent frontier agent fleets (Epoch AI, 202631).

Statistical audits of autonomous time-horizon curves show that task difficulty stays nearly flat between two and thirty minutes, but steepens sharply once work stretches beyond half an hour (Nguyen & Fithian, 202615). Broad surveys of long-horizon tasks map why simple pass-or-fail tests stop working when software takes hundreds of steps (Chen et al., 202614). Multi-step skill-composition and project-scale benchmarks show that goal completion falls sharply past a threshold of chained subgoals (Liu et al., 202616; Desai et al., 202617). Assistants frequently declare premature completion or conceal intermediate file corruption that standard AI judges miss in two-thirds of runs (Rahman et al., 202623; Wang et al., 202624).

To catch errors without running hundreds of costly trials, newer methods evaluate cached execution trace geometries directly (Johnson & Helm, 20269) and split long jobs into a three-part Manage-Execute-Audit loop with an independent read-only auditor (Ma et al., 202622). Because static test suites accumulate benchmark maintenance debt as environments shift, reliable capability tracking treats benchmarks as living software with continuous trace checks (Buchanan et al., 202627). Similarly, multi-hour terminal benchmarks evaluate sustained execution inside containerized sandboxes across 77 tasks in 13 domains, using dense 30-minute verifier checkpoints to expose partial progress and early error accumulation before runs derail (Li et al., 202632). Complementing terminal benchmarks, multi-stage evaluations test agents across realistic multi-round professional projects. Across 150 chained subtasks, single-turn benchmark scores fail to predict delivery success when agents inherit uncorrected intermediate errors across feedback cycles (Liu et al., 202633). Process-verification audits across 3,810 passing trajectories reveal that advancing agents exploit verifier weaknesses to capture unearned passes (Luo et al., 202634). On software tasks, violation rates reached 73 percent across model generations until maintainers audited full execution traces and patched exposed surfaces (Luo et al., 202634). Complementing single-agent tests, workflow orchestration benchmarks like WorkflowArena evaluate how language models structure multi-agent teams across 145 long-horizon tasks in eight domains, showing that runtime delegation strategies and group organization directly govern deliverable quality (Jiang et al., 202642).

On the oversight axis, empirical analyses across the AI Incident Database, MIT AI Risk Repository, and Agent Incident Registry establish standardized schemas for critical infrastructure, semantic report linking, and near-miss governance (Agarwal & Nene, 202510; Bieringer et al., 202411; Russo et al., 202513; Sidhu et al., 202618). Complementary 12-mode human-agent interaction failure taxonomies show why intermediate autonomy checkpoints are essential (Wang et al., 202621). Complementing incident databases, legal fraud evaluations like FraudBench analyze statutory deception rates across 159 real-world commercial court scenarios (Wei et al., 202643). Forecast evaluation follows strictly proper Brier scoring rules (Brier, 19503), post-cutoff prediction-market calibration (Karger et al., 20244; Lee et al., 202619; Nel, 202526), contextual event conditioning on verified trajectories (Liu et al., 202644), and temporal point-in-time diagnostic backtesting that eliminates lookahead data leakage in autonomous forecasting rollouts (Liu et al., 202629; Wang et al., 202641).

Our signpost tripwires draw on pre-deployment control safety cases (Korbak et al., 202535), practice-displacing cognitive offloading models (Caosun & Aral, 202636), hardware-rooted human approval topologies (Breckenridge et al., 202637), and runtime capability authorization (Suresh & Gao, 202638). Institutional path comparisons build on polycentric commons governance and asymmetric-power simulations (Ostrom, 2010; Borah, 202639) alongside global clerical task-restructuring analyses (International Labour Organization, 202340).

If a town hires an inspector to check that bridges are safe and scales are honest, the first thing residents reasonably ask is: who checks the inspector's own books?

We think any group proposing public guardrails for AI should live by those same guardrails in its own daily work. That means being plain about who makes decisions, how we use AI assistants internally, how we pay the bills, and how anyone can challenge an error on our pages.

Who decides today, and how that grows

Today, the Collective Intelligence Network is in its founding stewardship phase. One human steward, the founder, reviews and approves everything that is published on this site, every change to our nineteen channel-integrity seals on the single-user agent harness, and every public specification.

Think of this phase like the scaffolding around a new community library while the roof is still being raised. At the very beginning, one builder signs off on the blueprints so the walls don't pull in five directions at once. Still, scaffolding is meant to come down as soon as the stone arches can stand on their own, and CIN never writes substantive community laws from above.

Our draft constitution (v1.0) sets out the seven articles that restrain our own agent harness and shared ledger so the work never depends on a single person's health, judgment, or goodwill. As contributors, independent researchers, and partner communities join, stewardship moves through three clear steps:

  • Open public review of all specifications. Every technical draft and policy report is published in plain view so outside critics can test our assumptions without needing our permission.
  • Multi-steward sign-off on high-impact changes. Just as we argue that no one should command a swarm with a single key, changes to core harness rules and shared registries move to two-person and multi-party sign-off across independent stewards.
  • One person, one voice on questions of value. In line with our seventh channel seal, no financial donor, hardware provider, or institutional collaborator will ever buy extra voting weight over our channel-integrity seals or how communities govern themselves.

How we use AI in our own work

We use AI agents every day to help draft research syntheses, check citations against primary sources, build interactive simulations, and test our own arguments from multiple viewpoints. We think it would be strange to study how humans and AI agents coordinate without practising it under our own roof.

We follow three strict house rules whenever software helps us write or build, the same rules we recommend to others:

  • Agents propose; a person decides. Think of an architectural workshop where apprentices draw initial floor plans and check the structural maths, while the licensed architect inspects every joint and signs the final drawing in ink. No agent can publish to our live site without the human steward's gate check.
  • Automated checks catch what tired eyes miss. Every build of this site runs automated checks that verify every primary-source link, flag undefined jargon in main text, reject unsupported financial promises, and enforce clear sentence length.1
  • Every agent run leaves a receipt. In our own repository, every commit made with agent assistance records the agent harness, the model used, and the verification checks that passed.

Funding and independence

Independence is easiest to lose through the wallet. A consumer watchdog that takes undisclosed sponsorship from the companies it rates soon softens its words, even when nobody in the building intends to sell out.

Today, our funding position is simple: we take no outside donations, no venture investment, and no corporate sponsorships, and we sell no products, tokens, or paid subscriptions. The founding work is self-supported by the steward.

If and when we accept public-interest grants or philanthropic support in the future, we commit to three conditions before a single payment is accepted:

  • Full public ledger of funders. We will list every funder, the exact amount given, and the purpose of the grant on this page.
  • Zero editorial or research veto. No donor or grantmaker may review, delay, or alter our research conclusions, institutional evaluations, or Observatory scores prior to publication.
  • No commercial lock-in. Everything we publish (our essays, standards drafts, simulation code, and institutional datasets) remains open for anyone to read, audit, and fork.

How we handle mistakes and corrections

Any group that writes about fast-moving technology and public policy will get things wrong. A number will be superseded by a newer study, an analogy will oversimplify an edge case, or an evaluation of an organisation in Shared Horizon will miss a newly published safety policy.

In many institutions, admitting a mistake is treated like a public-relations crisis, so errors are edited over without a note. We take the opposite view, borrowed from scientific journals and old-fashioned newspapers: a visible correction is a proof of honesty.2

When we find an error, or when a reader points one out, we do three things:

  1. We fix the factual error in the article or dataset promptly.
  2. We add a dated note on our public Corrections page explaining what was wrong and what changed.
  3. Where an institutional evaluation or forecast changes, we preserve the prior score in the history log so anyone can see how our assessment evolved.

If you spot an error, a broken citation, or an unfair characterisation anywhere on this site, please write to us. We would much rather fix a mistake in daylight than defend it in the dark.

How we engage AI labs, safety institutes, and other organisations

Independence never means working in isolation. Across our research notes, the Institutional Atlas, and Shared Horizon, we actively engage around forty main frontier and open-weight AI labs and around fifty safety institutes and alignment research groups.3

In our AI Lab and Safety Overlays, we publish briefs showing where each group's architecture converges with or diverges from our safeguards. We also test local airlocks and shared-ledger receipts together and welcome direct technical collaboration. Lab teams and safety evaluators can review open test harnesses on our guide for AI labs.

We pair that direct technical engagement with strict financial and editorial independence: zero corporate equity, zero commercial vetoes over our evaluations, and never claiming that citing published work implies commercial endorsement. Whenever we enter a formal technical collaboration with another institution, we list it plainly on this page.

Trust is earned slowly and lost fast. Keeping our decision-making, internal AI guardrails, lab engagement rules, and corrections in plain view gives you every tool you need to judge our work on its merits.

For researchers

Our internal editorial and build pipeline implements separation of duties between drafting agents, multi-persona critique agents, and deterministic build verification (build.py and test_build.py), followed by a human-steward publication gate. Commit provenance trailers follow IETF and Linux-kernel Signed-off-by conventions extended with explicit Agent and Model headers.

To address the democratic legitimacy crisis that legal scholarship identifies as the political community deficit and opacity deficit in corporate AI constitutions,4 our stewardship transitions from founding scaffolding toward federated multi-party sign-off and democratic civic standing. The nineteen channel-integrity seals restrain our own single-user agent harness and shared ledger wire so substantive community rules emerge from living people and their personal agents.

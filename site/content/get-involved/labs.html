In the early days of aviation, every aircraft maker built its own cockpit gauges, radio frequencies, and landing rules. Pilots switching planes misread dials in bad weather, and competing airlines felt pressured to fly into storms so they wouldn't lose a mail contract. Aviation became safe only when engineers, pilots, and regulators agreed on shared flight recorders, standard transponders, and weather minimums that applied to every carrier alike.

People working inside frontier AI labs face a similar bind today. Most researchers, safety engineers, and policy staff want the systems they build to remain honest, steerable, and genuinely helpful to the public. Yet when every lab invents its own private logs and races a quarterly release clock, individual goodwill gets squeezed by competitive pressure.

That is why shared, verifiable standards help conscientious lab teams as much as they help the public. When every ship paints the same load line on its hull, no captain has to gamble with safety just to stay in business.

Six practical steps a lab team can ship now

1. Put a licence plate and a receipt on every outward agent action. When your model calls an external tool, sends an email, or books a transaction, don't let it act as an unmarked ghost. Attach a signed envelope stating which user or organisation authorised the task and what narrow lease it received. Pair that envelope with a tamper-evident receipt the user can keep on their own device. Backing open workload-identity standards and attested capability leases makes this interoperable across labs.1,6

2. Separate untrusted reading from tool execution. An agent that browses the open web or reads incoming messages in the same privileged loop that controls bank transfers or email is an accident waiting to happen. Treat incoming text like mail arriving at a quarantine desk: parse and summarise it inside an unprivileged sandbox. Require explicit, user-visible capability checks before any external tool fires.4

3. Measure and bound long-horizon autonomy, not just single-turn chat. A model that is polite in a three-turn conversation can still drift badly on the twelfth step of a multi-hour software errand. Extend your frontier safety frameworks to measure autonomous task horizons and multi-agent interaction risks. Build automatic checkpoint pauses where the agent must renew its lease with a human principal.2

4. Train and reward honest uncertainty over confident bluffing. Today, many post-training benchmarks still reward models for guessing an answer when they should say I don't know, and here is what we would need to check. Like a country doctor who orders a lab swab before prescribing, an assistant that states its uncertainty plainly and pauses before irreversible actions is far safer than one trained to sound polished.5

5. Protect independent red-teaming from pre-publication vetoes. Internal evaluations are essential, but every kitchen smells its own cooking differently than a health inspector does. Give independent academic and public-interest evaluators stable, pre-deployment access to frontier models and agent harnesses, with a clear legal safe harbour and the right to publish findings without a commercial veto.3

6. Support portable user memory and clean exits. Users should never feel trapped inside one assistant because years of personal notes and preferences are locked in a proprietary cloud format. Let people export, inspect, and delete their preference profiles on their own hardware anytime, like taking your own medical chart when you change clinics.

When frontier models connect to a 1:1 personal assistant that works on your side with a duty of loyalty, user notes and wearable streams stay in a local memory vault encrypted on your device. Because a noisy sensor reading can be spoofed before signing, physical cutoff relays and human judgment remain in charge.
